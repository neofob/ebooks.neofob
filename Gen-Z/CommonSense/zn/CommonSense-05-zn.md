# ON AMERICA'S CURRENT POWER LEVEL, WITH SOME EXTRA THOUGHTS.

I've never met a single person, in England or America, who hasn't admitted that a split between our countries is
inevitable. Where we've actually lacked judgment is in trying to debate *when* the continent is "ripe" or "ready"
for independence.

Since everyone agrees it has to happen and only argues about the timing, let's look at the big picture to find
the exact moment. But honestly, we don't even have to look far—the moment is already here. The total synergy,
the absolute unity of everything, proves it.

Our strength isn't in our numbers, but in our unity; still, our current numbers are enough to shut down the rest
of the world. Right now, this continent has the largest body of trained and disciplined soldiers of any power
under heaven. We've hit that perfect sweet spot where no single colony can survive on its own, but together, we
can get it done. Any more or less than this, and it might actually be a disaster. Our land force is already locked
in. As for the navy, let's be real: Britain would never let us build a single warship as long as we're under her
thumb. We wouldn't be any further ahead a hundred years from now than we are today; actually, we'd be worse off,
because our timber is disappearing every day, and what's left will be too far away and too hard to get.

If the continent were overcrowded, these conditions would be unbearable. The more port towns we had, the more we'd
have to defend and the more we'd have to lose. But our current population is perfectly balanced with our needs;
nobody has to be idle. The drop in trade just gives us an army, and the needs of an army create a brand new trade.

We have zero debt. And whatever we borrow for this cause will be a glorious reminder of our grit. If we can leave
the next generation with a stable government and an independent constitution, the price is a steal. But spending
millions just to get a few trash laws repealed and kick out the current ministry is a waste. Doing that is just
cruel to our kids; it leaves them with the actual hard work to do and a massive debt on their backs for zero
benefit. That's not the move of an honorable person; it's the mark of a narrow-minded, low-tier politician.

Any debt we take on doesn't even matter if the job gets done. No nation should be completely debt-free. A national
debt is basically a national bond; and when it doesn't have interest, it's not a problem. Britain is drowning
in a debt of over 140 million pounds, paying 4 million in interest. In exchange for that debt, she has a huge
navy. America has no debt and no navy; but for a fraction of England's debt, we could have a navy that's twice as
big. The entire English navy isn't even worth more than 3.5 million pounds right now.

No country on earth is as perfectly set up to build a fleet as America. Tar, timber, iron, and rope are all grown
right here. We don't need to import anything. The Dutch make bank by renting their warships to the Spanish and
Portuguese, but they have to import almost everything they use. We should see shipbuilding as a business; it's
the natural industry of this country. It's the best investment we can make. A navy is worth more than it costs to
build. It's that perfect balance where trade and protection meet. Let's build; if we don't need them, we can sell
them and trade our paper money for actual gold and silver.

Regarding the crew, people get it wrong. You don't need a quarter of the men to be professional sailors. The
"Terrible" privateer, Captain Death, fought the hardest battle of the last war with fewer than twenty sailors on
board, even though he had over two hundred men. A few pro sailors can quickly teach a bunch of active land-dwellers
how a ship works. We've never been more ready to start on the sea than now, while our timber is still standing,
our fisheries are blocked, and our shipwrights are out of work. Huge warships with 70 and 80 guns were built in
New England forty years ago—why not now? Shipbuilding is America's biggest flex, and eventually, we'll be the
best in the world. The big empires in the East are mostly inland and can't compete. Africa is in chaos. No power
in Europe has as much coastline or as many materials. Nature gave America both. Russia's empire is almost shut
out from the sea, so their huge forests and minerals are just things they sell to others.

As for safety, do we really want to be without a fleet? We aren't the "little people" we were sixty years ago. Back
then, we could leave our stuff in the street and sleep without locking our doors. Things have changed, and our
defense needs to level up with our wealth. A random pirate could have sailed up the Delaware a year ago and shaken
down the city of Philadelphia for whatever he wanted. The same could happen anywhere. Some daring guy in a small
ship with 14 or 16 guns could rob the whole continent and walk away with half a million. These are the reasons we
need naval protection.

Some might say that once we make up with Britain, she'll protect us. Are we really that delusional? Do we want her
to keep a navy in our harbors just for that? Common sense says the power that tried to crush us is the absolute
worst choice to defend us. "Friendship" can be a front for conquest; after a long, brave fight, we could be tricked
into slavery. And if her ships aren't allowed in our harbors, how is she going to protect us? A navy three or
four thousand miles away is useless in an emergency. If we have to protect ourselves, why not just do it? Why pay
someone else to do it?

The English list of warships looks scary, but not even a tenth of them are actually ready for service. Some aren't
even built, but they keep the names on the list just for show—some ships are just a single plank remaining. And
of the ships that *are* ready, only a fifth can be sent to one place at a time. Britain's claims on the East and
West Indies, the Mediterranean, and Africa all drain their navy. Because of bias and ignorance, we've developed
a fake idea of the English navy, acting like we'd have to fight the whole thing at once. Some "secret Tories" use
this to scare us into not starting our own. That's a lie. If America had only a twentieth of Britain's naval power,
we'd still be an overmatch. Why? Because we don't claim any foreign lands; our whole force would be focused on
our own coast. We'd have a two-to-one advantage because the enemy has to sail thousands of miles to even hit us,
and then sail all that way back to refit. And while Britain can block our trade to Europe, we have the same hold
over her trade to the West Indies, which is right in our neighborhood and totally at our mercy.

We could even find a way to maintain a naval force during peacetime if we don't want a full-time navy. We could give
bonuses to merchants to build ships with 20, 30, 40, or 50 guns. Fifty or sixty of those ships, plus a few guardships,
would be enough. This avoids the English problem where the fleet just rots in the docks during peace. Combining
commerce and defense is a pro move; when our wealth and strength support each other, no external enemy can touch us.

We have everything we need for defense. Hemp is growing like crazy, so we've got rope. Our iron is better than
anywhere else. Our small arms are top-tier. We can cast cannons whenever we want. We're producing saltpeter and
gunpowder every day. Our knowledge is growing by the hour. Resolution is in our DNA, and courage has never left us.

So, what are we missing? Why are we hesitating? Britain offers nothing but ruin. If she's allowed back into the
government of America, this continent won't even be worth living in. Jealousy will always be there; riots will keep
happening. And who's going to stop them? Who's going to risk their life to force their own people to obey a foreign
power? The fight between Pennsylvania and Connecticut over some land shows how useless a British government is;
it proves that only a Continental authority can handle Continental matters.

Another reason why right now is the perfect time: we have fewer people, which means more unoccupied land. Instead
of the king giving that land to his useless yes-men, we can use it to pay off the current debt and support the
government. No nation on earth has an advantage like this.

The "infant state" of the colonies isn't a weakness—it's a strength. We're numerous enough, but if we were more
populous, we might be less united. It's a pattern: the more crowded a country is, the smaller its armies are. As
population grows, trade grows, and people get too absorbed in making money to care about anything else. Commerce
kills the spirit of patriotism and military defense. History shows the bravest achievements always happen when a
nation is young. With the rise of commerce, England lost its spirit. The city of London, for all its millions,
just takes insults like a coward. The more people have to lose, the less they're willing to risk. The rich are
basically slaves to fear, submitting to royal power with the trembling energy of a lapdog.

Youth is when you build good habits, for nations and people. It might be impossible to form the continent into
one government fifty years from now. The massive variety of interests from trade and population would just create
chaos. Colony would fight colony. The powerful ones would look down on others, and the wise would regret that they
didn't unite sooner. Now is the time. The bonds formed in youth and the friendships made during a crisis are the
ones that last. Our current union has both: we're young and we've been through the struggle. Our unity has survived
the drama, and it's something our kids will be proud of.

This is also that one-time-only moment: the time when a nation forms its own government. Most nations blow this chance
and end up with laws forced on them by conquerors. Usually, they get a king first, and then a government. But the
charter—the rules—should come first, and then the people to run it. Let's learn from other nations' mistakes
and start this government from the right end.

When William the Conqueror took England, he gave them law at the point of a sword. Until we legally and officially
occupy the seat of government in America, we're at risk of some "lucky thug" taking it over and treating us the
same way. Then where's our freedom? Where's our property?

As for religion, it's the absolute duty of any government to protect everyone's conscience. That's the only "business"
government should have with religion. Stop being narrow-minded and selfish about your beliefs, and your fears will
vanish. Suspicion is for small souls and ruins a good society. I truly believe it's the will of the Almighty that
we have different religious opinions; it gives us more room to show Christian kindness. If we all thought the same,
our faith would have nothing to prove. To me, the different denominations are like children in the same family,
just with different first names.

On page 40, I mentioned the idea of a Continental Charter (just a hint, not a full plan). I'll bring it up again:
a charter is a solemn bond that the whole community enters into to protect the rights of every part—whether it's
religion, personal freedom, or property. A fair deal and an honest count make long-term friends.

I also mentioned the need for large and equal representation; nothing is more important. Having too few electors or
too few representatives is dangerous. But if the number is both small and unequal, it's a disaster. For example,
when the Associators' petition was before the Pennsylvania Assembly, only 28 members were there. Eight from
Bucks County voted against it; if seven from Chester had done the same, two counties would have ruled the whole
province. That's the danger. The way that House tried to grab power over the province's delegates is a warning:
don't trust power out of your own hands. They put together a set of instructions for the delegates that would've
embarrassed a schoolboy, and after a few people approved it, it was passed as the will of the entire colony. If
the colony knew how much that House hated necessary public measures, they wouldn't trust them for a second.

Sometimes, emergencies make things convenient that would become oppressive if they lasted. "Fast" isn't always
"right." When America was in crisis, appointing people from the Assemblies was the quickest and best move, and
that's what saved us from ruin. But since we'll probably always have a Congress, we need to think about how we
choose its members. I ask the experts on human nature: is "representation" and "election" too much power for one
group of people to have? When we plan for our kids, we have to remember that virtue isn't hereditary.

We often get the best lessons from our enemies when they make mistakes. Mr. Cornwall (one of the Lords of the
Treasury) roasted the New York Assembly because it only had 26 members, arguing that such a "trifling number"
couldn't represent the whole. We thank him for his accidental honesty. (If you want to understand why large and
equal representation is so important, read Burgh's political disquisitions.)

To wrap up: however strange it seems, or however much some people hate the idea, the only way to settle our affairs
quickly is an open and determined declaration of independence. Here's why:

First: Usually, when two nations fight, others step in as mediators to help reach a peace deal. But as long as
America calls herself a "subject" of Great Britain, no one can mediate. We're just stuck in a forever-fight.

Secondly: It's unrealistic to think France or Spain will help us if we're just using that help to fix the connection
with Britain. Those powers would lose out in the end.

Thirdly: As long as we claim to be British subjects, other nations see us as rebels. It's a dangerous precedent
for their own peace to have people in arms while calling themselves "subjects." We get the paradox, but the rest
of the world doesn't.

Fourthly: If we published a manifesto and sent it to foreign courts—explaining the misery we've endured, the
peaceful methods that failed, and how we were forced to break off all ties because the British court is cruel, while
assuring them we want peace and trade—that would do more for this continent than a ship full of petitions to Britain.

Under our current title as "British subjects," we're ignored abroad. The custom of every court is against us. We
won't be heard until we take our place as an independent nation.

This might feel weird and difficult at first, but like every other step we've taken, it'll become normal soon. Until
independence is declared, the continent will feel like a person who keeps putting off a chore they know they have
to do—they hate starting it, want it over with, and are haunted by the fact that it's necessary.
