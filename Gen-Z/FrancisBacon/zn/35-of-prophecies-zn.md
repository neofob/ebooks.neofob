# OF PROPHECIES (THE PREDICTION TRAP)

I'm not talking about divine prophecies or ancient oracles, but those "predictions" from the recent past that came
from "hidden causes." There are plenty of examples: the Pythoness telling Saul he'd be gone tomorrow, or Virgil's
verses about the Roman Empire. Seneca wrote about the discovery of America before it happened. There are stories
of dreams—like the one where a woman dreamed Jupiter bathed her father, and he ended up crucified in the sun
and rain. Or Philip of Macedon dreaming he sealed his wife's belly, but the soothsayer told him she was actually
pregnant because you don't seal empty vessels.

There's a whole list of these: Brutus's phantasm, Tiberius's comment to Galba, the prophecy that someone from Judea
would rule the world (which some said was the Savior, others said was Vespasian), or Domitian dreaming of a golden
head growing from his neck. Even Henry VI once predicted that a young Henry VII would wear the crown. There are
also the "trivial" ones, like the rhyme "When hempe is spunne, England's done," which people thought meant the
end of England after the monarchs Henry, Edward, Mary, Philip, and Elizabeth. It turned out the only thing that
"ended" was the name—England became Britain.

Then there was the prophecy about the "black fleet of Norway" (the Spanish Armada of 1588). Regiomontanus also
predicted that 1588 would be a "miraculous year." And then there's Cleon's dream about being eaten by a dragon,
which a sausage-maker "expounded" in a way that was just a joke.

My take? All of these should be "despised" as actual beliefs. They're basically just "winter talk by the fireside." But
that doesn't mean they aren't *useful* to talk about—some of them have caused a lot of mischief, which is why
there are laws against them.

Why do people believe them? Three reasons:
1. **Confirmation Bias:** People notice when a prediction hits, but totally ignore it when it misses.
2. **Probable Guesses:** Some "prophecies" are just really good guesses based on tradition or logic, but the person
presents it as "divination" to look cool. Like the "prediction" about the Atlantic—it was already likely that
there was land there, so they just turned a guess into a prophecy.
3. **The Big Lie:** Most of them are just "impostures"—fake predictions made up by "crafty brains" *after*
the event already happened.

Basically, it's all a game. Don't bet your life on a prophecy.
