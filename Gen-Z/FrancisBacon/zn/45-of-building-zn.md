# OF BUILDING (THE ARCHITECTURE GUIDE)

Houses are for living in, not for looking at. Use should always come before "uniformity" (symmetry). Leave the
"perfect" palaces to the poets—they can build them with zero cost.

First rule: don't build a fair house on a "bad seat." A bad seat isn't just unwholesome air; it's "unequal"
air. If you build in a dip surrounded by hills, the heat gets trapped and the wind swirls, so you'll have wild
temperature swings. Other "bad seat" signs: bad roads, bad markets, bad neighbors, no water, no wood, no shade,
or being too far (or too near) to the city. If you have multiple houses, sort them so that what one lacks, the
other has. Lucullus once roasted Pompey for his big, airy rooms by asking, "But how do you do in winter?" His
answer? "I'm as smart as a bird; I just change my house for the season."

The Model Palace:
You need two "sides" to the front: a **Banquet Side** (for feasts and triumphs) and a **Household Side** (for daily
living). They should look uniform from the outside but be different inside, joined by a stately tower in the middle.

- **Banquet Side:** One massive room (40ft high) upstairs, with a prep room underneath.
- **Household Side:** A hall and a chapel, with winter and summer parlors, a large underground cellar, and private
kitchens/pantries.
- **The Tower:** Two stories, with a railed top and statues.

Avoid putting the servants' dining room under the main rooms—otherwise, the smell of their food will come up
like a tunnel.

The Layout:
There should be a fair court with three sides of lower buildings and staircases in turrets at the corners. Don't
pave the court (it's too hot in summer and cold in winter); keep it as grass with side alleys.
- **Banquet return:** Stately galleries with cupolas and colored windows.
- **Household return:** Chambers for guests and bedrooms. Make sure there are rooms for both summer (shady) and
winter (warm). Avoid "all-glass" houses where you can't escape the sun or cold.
- **Windows:** "Imbowed" (curved) windows are great for private talks and blocking wind/sun.

Beyond the first court is an inward court surrounded by a garden. The ground floor should be a "grotto" or a place
of shade, and the upper floors should have open galleries on pillars to enjoy the garden's freshness. Add some
rich cabinets with crystalline glass and fountains.

Before the main front, you should have three preliminary courts: a plain green court, a more garnished one, and a
final square court with terraces and pillars. Keep the "offices" (service buildings) at a distance, connected by
low galleries.

The garden and the rest of the estate should be designed for true pleasure, not just "state and magnificence." Don't
just follow the advice of workmen—they'll just make it expensive without making it actually good.
