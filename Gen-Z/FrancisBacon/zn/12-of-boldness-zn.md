# OF BOLDNESS (THE FAKE-IT-TIL-YOU-MAKE-IT ENERGY)

This sounds like some basic grammar-school lesson, but it's actually worth a deep dive. Demosthenes was once asked
what the most important part of being an orator was. He answered: "Action." What's next? "Action." What's next
after that? "Action." He knew this best because he actually had zero natural talent for it. It's a weird move that
"Action" (which is basically just the performance/vibe) is ranked so high above actual invention or eloquence,
as if the performance is everything. But the reason is simple: most people are more foolish than they are wise,
so the things that appeal to the "foolish" part of the mind are the most powerful.

The same thing happens in the professional world. What's the key to success? "Boldness." What's second and
third? "Boldness." Now, boldness is basically the child of ignorance and low-tier character—it's way inferior to
actual skill. But it still fascinates and freezes people who are either shallow or scared (which is most people). It
even works on wise people when they're having a weak moment. That's why it does wonders in "popular" governments
(where the crowd decides), but less so with senators or princes. And it only works at the start; once a "bold"
person is already in power, the magic fades because boldness is a terrible way to keep a promise.

There are "snake oil salesmen" for the body, and there are "snake oil salesmen" for the state—people who promise
huge "cures" for society, maybe gets lucky a few times, but have zero actual science behind it. They can't keep it
up. Some bold people even pull a "Mahomet's miracle." Mahomet told the people he'd call a mountain to him. When the
mountain didn't move, he didn't even blink—he just said, "If the mountain won't come to Mahomet, Mahomet will go
to the mountain." These people can fail miserably at a huge promise, but if they have "maxed out" their boldness,
they just shrug it off and move on to the next thing.

To people with real judgment, bold people are just a joke to watch. Even the average person finds extreme boldness a
bit ridiculous, because it's almost always paired with some kind of absurdity. It's especially funny to see a bold
person lose their cool; their face just goes completely wooden and shrunken. When a shy person gets embarrassed,
their energy fluctuates; but a bold person just freezes—like a "stale" move in chess where it's not a checkmate,
but the game just can't move. (But that's more of a roast than a serious observation).

Here's the real takeaway: boldness is always blind. It doesn't see risks or downsides. That's why it's terrible
for giving advice (counsel), but great for getting things done (execution). The right way to use bold people is
to never put them in charge of the strategy, but to let them be the "seconds" who execute the plan. In planning,
you need to see the dangers; in execution, you're better off not seeing them unless they're absolutely massive.
