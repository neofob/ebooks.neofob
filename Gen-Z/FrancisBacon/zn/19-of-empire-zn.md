# OF EMPIRE (THE STRESS OF THE CROWN)

It's a miserable vibe to have almost nothing left to want, but a million things to fear. That's basically the life of
a king. Since they're already at the top, they lack a "main goal," which makes their minds languish. Plus, they're
constantly haunted by shadows and perils, which makes their headspace cluttered. That's why the Bible says the
"king's heart is inscrutable"—because they're so full of jealousy and lack one dominant desire to organize everything.

This is why princes often manufacture "fake goals" and obsess over toys: a new building, starting a secret society,
promoting a random person, or trying to be the GOAT at a random hobby. Like Nero with the harp, Domitian with the
bow, Commodus with fencing, or Caracalla with chariot racing. People who don't get the psychology of it might find
this weird, but the human mind is actually more refreshed by making progress in "small things" than by staying
stagnant in "big things."

Kings who were massive conquerors in their youth often become superstitious and melancholy in their old age (like
Alexander the Great). When you're used to always moving forward and then you hit a wall, you stop liking yourself
and lose your identity.

The "correct vibe" of an empire is rare and hard to maintain. It's a balance of opposites. As Apollonius told
Vespasian: Nero could play the harp perfectly, but in government, he'd either "wind the pins too high" (too strict)
or "let them down too low" (too slack). Nothing destroys authority faster than inconsistent power—swinging from
"extreme control" to "zero control."

Modern princes focus more on "fine deliveries"—meaning they're just reacting to dangers as they appear rather
than having a solid plan to keep them away. This is just gambling with fortune. And no one can stop the "spark"
of a riot; you just don't know where it's coming from.

The hardest part of being a prince is often their own mind. Tacitus said princes often want contradictory things. It's
a "power glitch" to think you can command the end result without enduring the messy middle.

Princes have to manage a lot of different "NPCs": neighbors, wives, kids, clergy, nobles, merchants, commoners,
and the army. All of them are potential risks if you aren't paying attention.

- **Neighbors:** No general rule, but keep a constant watch. Make sure no neighbor "overgrows" (gets too much
land or trade) to the point where they can actually annoy you. This is why Henry VIII, Francis I, and Charles V
kept each other in check—if one won a palm of ground, the other two would immediately balance it out. Also, a
"just fear" of imminent danger is a valid reason for war, even if they haven't attacked yet.
- **Wives:** Some are straight-up dangerous. Livia (poisoned her husband), Roxolana (ruined Sultan Mustapha),
and the Queen of Edward II (deposed and murdered him). This usually happens when the wife is plotting for her own
kids or is cheating.
- **Children:** Also a huge risk. Distrusting your children is almost always a disaster. The destruction of Mustapha
ruined Solyman's line; the destruction of Crispus ruined Constantine's house. Except when the kids are actually
in open rebellion, distrusting them usually backfires.
- **Clergy:** Dangerous when they're too proud or answer to a foreign power (like the Pope), or when the people
get to elect them.
- **Nobles:** Keep them at a distance, but don't crush them. If you make a king "absolute" by destroying the
nobility, you might be more powerful, but you're less safe. Henry VII depressed his nobility, and as a result,
he had to do everything himself because no one would help him.
- **Second Nobles:** Not a big threat because they're scattered. They actually balance the high nobility and help
manage the common people.
- **Merchants:** They are the "main vein" of the state. If they aren't flourishing, the kingdom's "veins" are
empty. Taxing them too much usually fails, because the increase in taxes just kills the total volume of trade.
- **Commoners:** Not a threat unless they have a "strong head" leading them, or if you mess with their religion,
customs, or how they make a living.
- **The Army:** Super dangerous if they stay in one big group and get used to "donatives" (cash bribes), like the
Janissaries. To stay safe, train them in different places under different commanders and don't give them bribes.

Princes are like celestial bodies—they create the "weather" for everyone else. They get a lot of respect, but
they never get to rest. All the rules for kings fit into two reminders: "Remember you are human" (to bridle their
power) and "Remember you are a God/Vice-God" (to bridle their will).
