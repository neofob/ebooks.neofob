# OF SEDITIONS AND TROUBLES (THE STATE CRASH)

Leaders need to know the "weather forecast" for state turmoil. The biggest storms usually happen when things are
becoming too equal (like natural storms at the equinox). Just as there are weird wind gusts and secret swells
before a storm at sea, there are signs in a state.

Signs of trouble include:
1. Frequent and open "hit pieces" (libels) and toxic discourse against the state.
2. Fake news spreading fast and being believed instantly. Virgil said "Fame" was the sister of giants—meaning
rumors are the leftovers of past riots, but they're also the trailers for future ones. Seditious rumors and actual
riots are basically siblings. It's especially bad when the state's "best" and most "plausible" actions are twisted
into something toxic—that shows the hater energy is peaking.

But just because rumors are a sign of trouble doesn't mean you should crush them with too much severity. Often,
just ignoring them is the best way to kill them; trying to stop them just makes the drama last longer. Also,
be suspicious of "obedience" that looks like this: people who argue, excuse, or nitpick every order instead of
just doing it. That's a way of "shaking off the yoke." It's a huge red flag when those in charge speak softly and
tentatively, while those against them speak boldly and aggressively.

Also (as Machiavelli noted), when leaders start picking sides instead of being "parents" to everyone, the boat
tips over. If the authority of the prince becomes just a tool for a specific cause, and other bonds (like factional
loyalty) become stronger than the bond of sovereignty, the king basically loses his house.

When fights and factions are carried out openly and boldly, it's a sign that respect for the government is gone. The
movements of the top people should be like planets—driven by a higher motion and moving softly on their own. When
the "great ones" start moving violently and "freer than they remember who's in charge," the system is broken. Respect
is the "belt" that connects princes to God; without it, everything dissolves.

If any of the "four pillars" of government—religion, justice, counsel, and treasure—are shaken, it's time to
pray for better weather.

Let's talk about the "materials" of sedition. The surest way to stop a riot is to remove the fuel. The fuel for
sedition is two things: massive poverty and massive discontentment. A bankrupt state is a state ready to crash. If
the rich are broke and the poor are starving, the danger is imminent—"rebellions of the belly" are the absolute worst.

Discontentment is like a "toxic humor" in the body that can suddenly inflame. A leader shouldn't judge the danger
by whether the grievances are "just" or "unjust"—people aren't always reasonable and will often throw away
their own good. Also, don't judge by whether the griefs are "big" or "small." The most dangerous vibe is when
the *fear* is bigger than the *feeling*. In cases of extreme oppression, the same things that break your patience
can actually kill your courage. And don't feel safe just because discontentment has been around for a long time
without a riot. Every vapor doesn't become a storm, but storms eventually happen. As the Spanish proverb says:
"The cord breaks at the last by the weakest pull."

Triggers for sedition include: religious shifts, new taxes, changing laws, breaking privileges, general oppression,
promoting "mid" people, foreigners in power, food shortages, disbanded soldiers, and desperate factions. Basically,
anything that knits people together in a common "we're getting screwed" cause.

The remedies:
General prevención is to remove the material cause: poverty. This means opening and balancing trade, supporting
manufacturing, killing idleness, stopping excess via laws, improving the land, regulating prices, and moderating
taxes. Basically, make sure the population doesn't exceed the "stock" of the kingdom. Too many nobles or too many
clergy members who don't produce anything will bankrupt a state.

Remember that growth comes from foreigners (because if someone gains, someone else loses). A nation sells three
things: raw materials, manufactured goods, and transport (carriage). If these three wheels are turning, wealth
flows. Sometimes the "work and carriage" are worth more than the material itself—like in the Low Countries.

Good policy also means making sure money isn't all gathered in a few hands. A state can have a huge stock and
still starve. Money is like fertilizer—it's only good if it's spread around. This means stopping usury and the
hoarding of land.

To handle discontentment: there are the nobles and the common people. If only one is unhappy, the danger is low. Common
people move slowly unless the nobles excite them; nobles have no strength unless the crowd is ready to move. When the
nobles just wait for the water to get muddy among the commoners so they can make their move—that's the danger zone.

Giving people a moderate "vent" for their griefs (as long as they aren't being too arrogant) is a safe move. If
you force the "humors" back in, you create a malignant ulcer.

The best provision against discontentment is "Hope." Keeping people moving from one hope to another is the best
antidote to the poison of discontent. A wise government knows how to hold people by hope when it can't hold them
by satisfaction. Most people are happy to flatter themselves or pretend to believe in a hope, even if they don't.

Also, make sure there's no "fit head" for the discontented to rally around. A "fit head" is someone with reputation,
who is trusted by the unhappy party, and who is also unhappy themselves. These people should either be won over
quickly or countered by someone else from the same party to split the reputation. Generally, breaking up factions
and making them distrust each other is a solid move. If the government is full of discord but the opposition is
united, you're in trouble.

Finally, be careful what you say. Short, sharp speeches from princes can spark riots. Caesar did himself huge damage
with one comment about Sylla's literacy, which killed the hope that he'd ever give up his dictatorship. Galba ruined
himself by saying he "levied" his soldiers rather than "buying" them, which killed the hope of a bonus. In tense
times, princes should watch their words—short quotes fly like darts and are seen as "secret intentions." Long
speeches are too boring to be noted.

Lastly, always have one or more military leaders you trust close to you to crush seditions in the beginning. Without
them, the court will panic too much and the state will risk the "everyone is terrified" vibe described by
Tacitus. These military people should be respected and professional, not "factious" or "popular" politicians,
or the remedy will be worse than the disease.
