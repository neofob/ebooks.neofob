# OF DISPATCH (THE HUSTLE CULTURE)

Trying to force "dispatch" (speed) is one of the most dangerous things you can do to a project. It's like "hasty
digestion"—it just fills the body with "crudities" and seeds of disease. Don't measure speed by how many hours
you spend sitting in a meeting, but by how much the business actually *advances*. In a race, it's not about the
biggest stride, but about staying close to the matter and not trying to take too much at once.

Some people just want to "look" like they're fast. They create fake deadlines or abbreviate things by "cutting off"
parts of the work instead of "contracting" it. This just makes the project go backward and forward in an unsteady
way. One wise man used to say to people rushing for a conclusion: "Stay a little, so we can finish sooner."

On the other hand, *true* dispatch is a huge asset. Time is the currency of business, just as money is the currency
of goods. If you have low dispatch, you're paying a premium for your work. The Spartans and Spaniards were famous
for having zero dispatch—there's a joke: "Let my death come from Spain," because it'll take forever to get here.

How to actually be fast:
- Give the person giving the first report a fair hearing. Direct them at the start, rather than interrupting them in
the middle. If you mess up their flow, they'll just go back and forth in their memory and become tedious. (Sometimes
the "moderator" is more annoying than the speaker).
- Avoid useless repetitions. But *do* iterate the "state of the question" often—this kills off frivolous speeches
before they even start.
- Long, fancy speeches are like wearing a robe with a long train to a race—it just slows you down. Prefaces,
excuses, and "about me" introductions are a waste of time. They might look like "modesty," but they're actually just
"bravery" (showing off). However, if people are resistant or blocked, you *do* need a preface to "warm them up"
so the main point can enter.

The secret to dispatch is **Order and Distribution**. Divide the work into parts, but don't be too "subtle" about
it. If you don't divide, you'll never get into the business; if you divide too much, you'll never get out. Picking
the right time is the same as saving time.

Business has three parts:
1. Preparation.
2. Debate/Examination.
3. Perfection.
If you want speed, let the *middle* part be the work of many, but the *first* and *last* parts should be the work
of a few. Also, starting with a written proposal makes everything faster—even if the proposal is rejected, a
"no" is more useful than an "I don't know."
