# OF THE TRUE GREATNESS OF KINGDOMS AND ESTATES (THE POWER SCALE)

Themistocles had a huge ego, but he made a great point: he couldn't play the lute, but he could turn a small town
into a great city. This shows two different skill sets in government. Some people can actually make a state great,
but they can't "fiddle." Others can "fiddle" perfectly—meaning they're great at the "degenerate arts" and "shifts"
that get them favor with their bosses and the crowd—but they're actually useless for the long-term growth of the
state. Then there are the "mid" governors: they can manage things and keep them from crashing, but they don't have
the vision to actually amplify a state's power.

The "true greatness" of a state isn't just about the numbers. You can measure territory and revenue, and you
can count the population and the cities. But judging a state's actual *power* is the hardest part. The Kingdom
of Heaven is compared to a mustard seed—tiny, but it has a spirit that spreads fast. Some states are huge in
territory but can't command anything; others are small but become the foundations of great empires.

Having walled towns, armories, fancy horses, and artillery is just a "sheep in a lion's skin" unless the people
themselves are stout and warlike. Huge armies don't mean much if the people have zero courage. Virgil said,
"A wolf doesn't care how many sheep there are." Alexander the Great didn't "pilfer the victory" by attacking the
Persians at night—he did it in broad daylight because he knew their numbers didn't matter. Tigranes laughed at
the Romans because they only had 14,000 men against his 400,000—but by sunset, he was the one running. The most
important point of greatness is having a "race of military men."

Money isn't the "sinews of war" if the people are too "effeminate" to fight. As Solon told Croesus, if someone
comes with better iron, they'll take all your gold. So, a state should only trust its strength if it has native,
valiant soldiers. Relying on mercenaries is a temporary fix—you might "spread your feathers" for a bit, but
you'll lose them soon after.

Also, you can't have a warlike people if they're crushed by taxes. People who are overcharged with tribute aren't
fit for empire. However, taxes that are agreed upon by the state don't hurt courage as much.

To stay great, a state must make sure its nobility and "gentlemen" don't multiply too fast. If there are too many
"high-tier" people, the common subjects become just "peasants" and "laborers." It's like a forest: if the plants
are too thick, you never get clean undergrowth. In England, the "middle people" make good soldiers, whereas the
peasants in France don't. Henry VII was a genius here—he set a standard for farms so that the owners could live in
"convenient plenty" and not in a servile condition. This keeps the plow in the hands of owners, not hirelings.

Also, having a culture of "free servants" and high hospitality among nobles helps build military strength. If
nobles live in a "closed and reserved" way, they end up with a shortage of forces.

A state also needs a "strong trunk" to support its "branches"—meaning the native citizens must be a sufficient
proportion compared to the foreigners they govern. States that are liberal with naturalization (like Rome) are
fit for empire. Rome didn't just spread over the world; the world spread over the Romans because they granted
citizenship and rights (voting, honor, etc.) to entire families and nations. Spain also managed this by employing
all nations in its militia.

Certain arts—like "sedentary" or "delicate" manufactures that require fingers more than arms—actually clash
with a military vibe. Warlike people tend to be a bit idle and love danger more than hard work. Ancient states
like Sparta and Rome used slaves for the "delicate" work. The modern move is to leave those arts to foreigners
and keep the natives as farmers, free servants, and "manly" craftsmen (smiths, masons, carpenters).

Above all, a nation must make "arms" their principal honor and study. Romulus told the Romans that if they focused on
arms, they'd be the greatest empire in the world. The Turks and Spaniards have seen this. Any nation that doesn't
directly profess arms can't expect greatness to just "fall into their mouths."

Also, a state needs "just occasions" for war. You can't just attack for no reason. The Turks use "spreading their
law" as a reason. Romans were sensible about wrongs to their borderers or merchants. A great state is always awake
and ready to help its allies.

Finally, a "just and honorable war" is like exercise for the state—it keeps the body healthy. Civil war is like
a fever, but foreign war keeps the courage from becoming "effeminate." Having a veteran army on foot is what gives
a state its reputation.

And don't forget the sea. Commanding the sea is a "shortcut" to monarchy. Whoever controls the sea has the most
liberty. Great Britain's biggest "dowowry" is its sea power, which lets it access the wealth of the Indies.

Ancient wars were all about glory and honor—triumphs, monuments, and huge rewards. Modern wars are a bit "darker"
and less about the individual's glory. But the Roman triumph was a based institution: it gave honor to the general,
money to the treasury, and rewards to the army.

To wrap it up: just as a man can't "add a cubit to his stature" by worrying, a prince *can* add greatness to his
kingdom by introducing the right laws and customs. Most just leave it to chance, but the ones who plan for their
posterity are the ones who actually win.
