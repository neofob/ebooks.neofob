# OF ADVERSITY (THE CHARACTER ARC)

Seneca had a high-key take: "The good things that come with winning are things you want, but the good things
that come from the struggle are things you admire." If miracles are about hacking nature, they show up most when
things are going south. He even went harder, saying it's true greatness to have the fragility of a human but the
untouchable energy of a God. This would've hit better as poetry, where you can be extra. The ancient poets had this
weird story about Hercules sailing the ocean in a clay pot to save Prometheus—which is basically a metaphor for
the Christian vibe: sailing in the frail boat of the flesh through the chaotic waves of the world.

To put it simply: the virtue of winning is temperance, but the virtue of struggling is fortitude—and in the real
world, fortitude is the more heroical energy. Prosperity is the blessing of the Old Testament, but adversity is the
blessing of the New, which carries the bigger glow-up and a clearer sign of God's favor. Even in the Old Testament,
if you listen to David's harp, there are as many funeral vibes as there are celebration songs; and the Holy Spirit
spent way more time describing Job's trauma than Solomon's riches.

Winning isn't without its own anxieties and icks, and struggling isn't without its comforts and hopes. Think about
embroidery: it's way more aesthetic to have a bright, lively design on a dark, solemn background than a dark
design on a bright one. Judge the pleasure of the heart the same way you judge the pleasure of the eye. Virtue
is like a high-end perfume—it smells the strongest when it's crushed or burned. Prosperity exposes your flaws,
but adversity exposes your strength.
