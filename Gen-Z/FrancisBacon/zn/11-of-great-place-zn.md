# OF GREAT PLACE (THE GOLDEN CAGE)

People in high positions are basically triple-servants: servants of the state, servants of their own fame, and
servants of their endless to-do lists. They have zero freedom—not in their bodies, not in their actions, and
definitely not in their time. It's a strange vibe: seeking power only to lose your liberty; seeking power over
others only to lose power over yourself.

The climb to the top is a struggle, and you just trade small pains for bigger pains. Sometimes the climb is toxic,
and you have to do some mid things to get to a high position. The top is slippery, and the way down is either a
total crash or an "eclipse," which is a mood. Once you're there, you can't even retire when you want to, and you
can't stand being "just a regular person," even when you're old and sick. You're like an old townie who sits at
their door all day, even though they're just making themselves a target for mockery.

High-status people have to borrow other people's opinions to feel happy. If they judge by their own feelings,
they can't find the happiness. But if they think about how other people see them—and how everyone wants to be
them—then they're "happy by report," even if they're miserable inside. They are the first to feel their own
grief, but the last to realize their own faults. People with great fortunes are basically strangers to themselves;
they're so lost in the puzzle of business that they forget to take care of their mental and physical health.

In a position of power, you have the license to do good or evil—and the latter is a curse. With evil, the best
state is not wanting to do it; the second best is not being able to. But the power to do good is the only real
reason to strive for the top. Good intentions are just "good dreams" unless they're put into action, and you can't
do that without the "vantage ground" of power. Merit and good works are the goal of the human hustle, and a clean
conscience is the ultimate rest.

When you're in power, look at the best examples. Imitating a GOAT is like a shortcut to a thousand lessons. After a
while, look at your own early examples and see if you've stayed true to your original vibe. Also, study the people
who failed in the same role—not to roast them, but to know what to avoid. Reform things without being arrogant
or shading the people who came before you. Try to create good precedents.

Go back to the basics and see how things went wrong. Get advice from both eras: from the ancients, find what's
best; from the moderns, find what's most fit. Make your process predictable so people know what to expect, but
don't be too rigid. If you have to break your own rules, explain why clearly. Keep the rights of your office,
but don't start "jurisdiction wars." Just take your rights in silence and actually use them, rather than shouting
about your claims. Also, respect the rights of the people below you; it's a bigger flex to lead the whole thing
than to micro-manage every tiny detail. Welcome help and advice on how to execute your role; don't treat people
who give you info as "meddlers," but appreciate them.

The four main "fails" of authority are: delays, corruption, roughness, and being too "easy" (facility).
- **For delays:** Give people easy access, keep your appointments, and finish what you started. Don't multitask
unless you absolutely have to.
- **For corruption:** Don't just tell your staff not to take bribes; make it so the people offering them can't
even try. Integrity that is practiced is one thing, but integrity that is loudly proclaimed with a "disgust" for
bribery stops the problem at the source. Avoid the fault, and avoid the *suspicion* of the fault. If you change
your mind or course without a clear reason, people will assume you've been bought. Always be transparent about
why you're changing your mind. If you have a "favorite" who is close to you for no apparent reason, people will
assume they're a backdoor for bribes.
- **For roughness:** This is just a useless way to make people hate you. Severity creates fear, but roughness
creates hate. Even when you have to roast someone from a position of power, keep it grave, not taunting.
- **For being too easy (facility):** This is actually worse than bribery. Bribes only happen sometimes, but if you're
just "too nice" or let people use your relationships to get things done, it never stops. As Solomon said, respecting
people based on who they are instead of what's right is a fail—such a person would sell out for a piece of bread.

It's true that "a position shows the man." It makes some people look better and some look worse. Tacitus said
Galba looked like he could lead until he actually did; but Vespasian actually became a better person once he was
in power. It's a sign of a great spirit when honor actually improves the person. Virtue in the "hunting" phase
(ambition) is violent, but once it's in power (authority), it becomes settled and calm.

The climb to the top is always a "winding stair." If there are factions, it's smart to pick a side while you're
rising, but balance yourself once you're at the top. Be fair to your predecessor's memory; if you don't, that
debt will be paid when you're gone. Respect your colleagues; call them in when they don't expect it rather than
excluding them when they do. Don't be too "stuck up" about your position in private conversations; let people say,
"When he's in the office, he's a different man."
