# OF COUNSEL (THE INNER CIRCLE)

The biggest trust you can have with another human is the trust of giving counsel. In other situations, you might
trust someone with your land, your money, or your kids—but when you make someone your counselor, you're trusting
them with *everything*. That's why counselors must be 100% real.

The smartest leaders don't think it's a "skill issue" or a sign of weakness to rely on counsel. Even God has a
name for his Son: "The Counsellor." Solomon said that "in counsel is stability." If you don't test your ideas
against arguments, they'll just be tossed around by the waves of fortune—doing and undoing things like a drunk
person. Solomon's son learned the hard way that bad counsel ruins everything; the two red flags for bad counsel are:
the person is too young/inexperienced, and the advice is too "violent" or extreme.

The ancients used a metaphor: Jupiter married Metis (who represents Counsel). Then Jupiter got pregnant with Metis
and "ate her up," and eventually gave birth to Pallas Athena (the goddess of wisdom) from his own head. This is a
secret of empire: first, a king should refer matters to his council (the "impregnation" phase). But once the idea
is molded and ready, he shouldn't let the council make the final call. He takes it back into his own hands and
presents the final decision as his own "head and device." This adds to his reputation—the final decree comes
from his own power and prudence.

There are three main downsides to having a council:
1. Affairs become less secret.
2. The prince looks weaker, as if he can't decide on his own.
3. The risk of "fake" counsel—where the advisor is just looking out for their own bag.

Some people try to fix this with "cabinet councils" (tiny, secret groups), but that's often a worse remedy than
the disease.

Regarding secrecy: a prince doesn't have to tell *every* counselor *everything*. He can pick and choose. Also,
just because you're asking for advice doesn't mean you have to reveal what you've already decided to do. The
biggest leak is usually the prince himself, or one "loudmouth" in a cabinet council who thinks it's a flex to
tell everyone's secrets. Some things *do* require extreme secrecy—just one or two people. That actually works
well because the direction stays consistent. But this requires a prince who can "grind with a hand-mill" (handle
things himself) and counselors who are truly loyal. King Henry VII was like this—he only trusted Morton and Fox.

Regarding authority: using a council actually *increases* the majesty of a king. He only loses power if one
counselor becomes too dominant or if the council forms a "bloc" against him.

Regarding loyalty: you'll never find perfect faith everywhere, but some people are naturally sincere and
direct. Princes should hunt for those types of people. Also, counselors usually keep an eye on each other; if one
is acting out of faction or greed, the others will usually leak it to the king. The best move is for the prince
to know his counselors as well as they know him.

Counselors shouldn't try to "analyze" the prince's personality too much. Their job is to be an expert in the
*business*, not the *person*. If you focus on the person, you just end up "feeding their humor" (telling them what
they want to hear).

Pro tip for princes: get opinions both separately and together. Private opinions are more honest; public opinions
are more formal and respectful. Get the "low-tier" staff's opinions in private to keep them free, and the "high-tier"
staff's opinions in a group to keep the respect.

Also, don't just seek counsel on *what* to do, but on *who* should do it. A great plan is a "dead image" without
the right people to execute it. The biggest mistakes happen when picking individuals, not categories. As they say,
"The best counselors are dead"—meaning books are often clearer than living advisors. So, read books by people
who actually did the work.

Modern councils are often just "chat sessions" where people talk but don't actually debate. They rush to a
decision. It would be better to propose a major issue one day and not discuss it until the next—"In the night
is counsel." This was done in the union of England and Scotland. Also, set specific days for petitions so the main
meetings can focus on "state business."

When forming committees, choose "indifferent" (neutral) people rather than trying to balance "strong sides." Standing
commissions (for trade, war, etc.) are also a great move. Experts (lawyers, sailors, etc.) should be heard by
committees first, not in a giant crowd, to avoid "clamoring" instead of "informing."

Also, the shape of the table matters. A long table gives too much power to the people at the top. A square table
or sitting around the walls allows more a variety of opinions to be heard. Finally, when a king presides, he
should avoid showing his own preference too early—otherwise, the counselors will just "sing a song of placebo"
and agree with him just to stay in favor.
