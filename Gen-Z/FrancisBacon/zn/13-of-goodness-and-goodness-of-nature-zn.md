# OF GOODNESS, AND GOODNESS OF NATURE (THE PURE VIBE)

When I talk about "goodness," I mean actually caring about the well-being of other people (what the Greeks called
*philanthropia*). "Humanity" is a bit too mid of a word to describe it. I call the habit of doing it "goodness,"
and the natural urge to do it "goodness of nature." Of all the virtues and mental flexes, this is the biggest
one—it's literally the character of God. Without it, a human is just a busy, toxic, miserable thing—basically
a pest. Goodness is the "charity" of theology, and you can't have "too much" of it; any "excess" is just an
error. Thinking you have too much power made angels fall; thinking you knew too much made humans fall; but you
can't fall by being too charitable.

The urge to be good is coded deep into human nature. If it doesn't come out toward other humans, it'll come
out toward animals. Even people known for being cruel can be kind to beasts—like some reports of people in
Constantinople who almost stoned a boy for messing with a bird.

Of course, people can mess up this virtue. There's a salty Italian proverb: "So good that he's good for nothing." And
Machiavelli had the nerve to write that the Christian faith just leaves good people as prey for tyrants. He
said this because no other religion has amplified the value of goodness as much as Christianity. To avoid the L,
it's important to know the "errors" of this habit. Seek the good of others, but don't be a slave to their faces
or their whims—that's just being "soft," and it turns an honest mind into a prisoner. Don't give a diamond to
someone who would be happier with a grain of barley.

God's example is the blueprint: "He sends rain and makes the sun shine on the just and the unjust," but he
doesn't give everyone the same amount of wealth, honor, or virtue. General benefits should be for everyone, but
special benefits should be for the chosen. And be careful not to break the pattern: the love of ourselves is the
blueprint, and the love of our neighbors is the copy. "Sell everything and follow me"—but don't sell everything
unless you actually *do* follow me; meaning, unless you have a calling where you can do a lot of good with very
little. Otherwise, if you try to feed every stream, you'll just dry up the fountain.

Some people have a natural disposition toward goodness, and some have a natural "malignity" (they just don't care
about others' well-being). The "light" version of this is just being contrary, grumpy, or difficult. The "deep"
version is envy and pure mischief. These people are only happy when others are crashing; they're like flies buzzing
around something raw. They're "misanthropes" who want to bring people to the end of their rope, even though they
don't even have a tree in their own garden to do it with. These dispositions are basically "bugs" in human nature,
but they're actually the best "timber" for high-level politics—like the curved wood used for ships that are
meant to be tossed around, but not for houses that need to stand firm.

There are many signs of a truly good person:
- If they're gracious to strangers, it shows they're a citizen of the world—their heart isn't an isolated island,
but a continent.
- If they feel others' pain, it shows their heart is like a noble tree that gets wounded itself while producing
the balm.
- If they pardon offenses easily, it shows their mind is "too high" to be hit by insults.
- If they're thankful for tiny things, it shows they value the person's heart, not their "trash."

But above all, if someone is willing to sacrifice their own standing with God for the salvation of others, that's
a divine nature—a total mirror of Christ.
