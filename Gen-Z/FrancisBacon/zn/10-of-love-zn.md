# OF LOVE (THE ULTIMATE DISTRACTION)

The theater loves love more than real life does. On stage, love is always the plot of comedies and occasionally
a tragedy. But in real life, it's often just chaos—sometimes like a Siren leading you to crash, sometimes like
a Fury burning everything down. If you look at the truly great and worthy people in history, almost none of them
ever went "full simp" for someone. This shows that great spirits and big goals usually block out this weak passion.

You have to make exceptions for Marcus Antonius (who shared the Roman Empire) and Appius Claudius. Antonius was
already a hedonist, but Appius was an austere, wise man. This proves that love can sneak into even a well-fortified
heart if you aren't paying attention. Epicurus had a mid take: "We are enough of a theater for each other," as
if humans—made to contemplate the heavens and noble things—should just kneel before a little idol and make
themselves subjects of their own eyes.

The way this passion warps the value of things is wild. Hyperbole (extreme exaggeration) is only acceptable in
love; in any other context, it's just cringe. And while it's true that we are our own biggest fans, a lover is
even worse—no proud person is as delusional about themselves as a lover is about the person they love. Truly,
it's impossible to be "in love" and "wise" at the same time. This weakness is obvious to everyone, especially to
the person being loved (unless they love you back). Basically, love is always rewarded with either a return of
the feeling or a secret, inward contempt. That's why you have to be careful—this passion doesn't just cost you
other things; it costs you your own dignity.

The poet describes these losses well: "He who chose Helena gave up the gifts of Juno and Pallas." Basically, if you
overvalue romantic love, you give up both wealth and wisdom. This passion hits hardest during times of weakness:
during peak success or peak failure. Both of these states kindle love, proving it's the child of folly.

The smartest move is to admit you love someone but keep it in its own lane—keep it completely separate from your
professional life and serious goals. If love starts interfering with business, it ruins your fortune and makes
you lose sight of your own ends.

Curiously, soldiers are very prone to love. I think it's like their love for wine—when you live a life of danger,
you want to be paid back in high-intensity pleasure.

Humans have a natural, secret inclination to love others. If it's not focused on one or a few people, it spreads
out and makes a person humane and charitable (like you see in some monks). Romantic love creates the human race;
friendly love perfects it; but "wanton" (toxic/obsessive) love corrupts and ruins it.
