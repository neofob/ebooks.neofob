# OF SIMULATION AND DISSIMULATION (THE ART OF THE MASK)

Playing the game (dissimulation) is actually just a low-tier version of strategy or wisdom. It takes a strong mind
and a strong heart to know when to keep it 100 and actually do it; the low-tier politicians are the ones who spend
all their time capping.

Tacitus said Livia was a pro at the arts of her husband and the masking of her son—giving the "strategy" credit
to Augustus and the "masking" to Tiberius. Strategy and masking are actually different skills. If you have the
judgment to know what to reveal, what to hide, and what to show in "half-light"—and to whom and when—then
having a habit of just lying is actually a nerf to your potential. But if you don't have that kind of judgment,
then your only option is to be generally closed-off and a dissembler. When you can't choose the specific move,
the safest bet is to just be wary in general, like someone walking softly because they can't see.

The most based people in history were usually open and frank, known for being real. But they were like well-trained
horses—they knew exactly when to stop or turn. When they actually needed to mask something, their reputation
for being 100 made them almost invisible.

There are three levels to this:
1. **Secrecy/Reservation:** When you just don't give away who you are.
2. **Negative Dissimulation:** When you drop hints that you're *not* who you actually are.
3. **Simulation:** When you actively pretend to be something you're not.

Secrecy is the vibe of a confessor; the secret person hears a lot of tea because nobody wants to open up to a
blabber. Being secret actually invites people to share their secrets, because the "closed" air pulls in the "open"
air. People just want to vent more than they want to actually bond. In short: mysteries require secrecy. Also, being
totally "emotionally naked" is a bit cringe; it adds more respect to a person's vibe if they aren't completely an
open book. People who talk too much are usually vain and believe anything; because if they tell you everything they
know, they'll also tell you things they *don't* know. So, being secret is both a strategic and a moral win. Also,
watch your face—your expressions can betray you way more than your words.

The second level (dissimulation) often happens because you have to. If you try to be perfectly neutral, people will
just keep grilling you with questions until you either stay silent (which is weird) or lean one way. "Equivocating"
or speaking in riddles doesn't work for long. To be truly secret, you need a little bit of masking as a shield.

The third level—simulation and fake professions—is the most problematic and the least strategic, unless it's
for something huge. A habit of pretending is usually a sign of a fake personality, fear, or some major flaws that
the person is trying to hide.

The pros of masking are: first, to put opposition to sleep and surprise them; second, to keep a "safe exit" (if you
don't announce your moves, you don't crash as hard if you fail); and third, to discover what others are thinking
(people are more likely to be real with someone who seems open). There's a Spanish proverb: "Tell a lie to find
a truth."

The cons are: first, it makes you look fearful, which ruins your momentum; second, it confuses people who might
have actually helped you; and third—the biggest L—it destroys trust and belief. The best balance is to have
a reputation for being open, keep your habits secret, mask only when needed, and fake it only as a last resort.
