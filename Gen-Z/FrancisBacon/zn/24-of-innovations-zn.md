# OF INNOVATIONS (THE UPDATE LOG)

Just like newborns are often "ill-shapen," every innovation starts out a bit messy. But the people who first bring
honor to their family are usually more "based" than those who just follow the pattern. The first good precedent
is rarely matched by imitation because human nature loves "continuance" (doing things the way they've always been
done), while "good" is a forced motion that's strongest at the start.

Every new medicine is an "innovation." If you refuse new remedies, you're just inviting new diseases. Time
is the ultimate innovator. If time naturally makes things worse, and we don't use wisdom to make them better,
we're cooked. It's true that things settled by custom might not be "good," but at least they're "fit" (they work
together). New things often feel like "strangers"—they're admired for their utility but disliked because they
don't fit the vibe.

But time doesn't stand still. Clinging to the past just because it's the past is just as chaotic as a random
innovation. People who over-reverence "the old days" are just a joke to the new generation.

If you're going to innovate, follow the example of Time itself: change things *quietly* and *by degrees* so people
barely notice. Otherwise, anything new is "unlooked for"—it helps some people (who thank the times) and hurts
others (who blame the author).

A few rules for innovating:
1. Don't experiment with a state unless it's an absolute emergency or the utility is obvious.
2. Make sure the "reformation" is what drives the change, not just a "desire for change" pretending to be a reformation.
3. Treat novelty with suspicion, even if you don't reject it. As the Scripture says, "Stand on the ancient way,
look around, find the straight path, and then walk in it."
