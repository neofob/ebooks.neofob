# OF FORTUNE (THE RNG OF LIFE)

Sure, "outward accidents" (random events) play a part in your fortune—favor, opportunity, someone else dying,
or a lucky break. But mostly, the "mould" of your fortune is in your own hands. As the poet said, "Everyone is
the architect of their own fortune." One of the most common ways to get lucky is when someone else is "mid"—the
folly of one man is the fortune of another.

Obvious virtues get you praise, but "hidden" virtues get you the bag. There's a Spanish word, "disemboltura," which
describes a nature that doesn't get stuck—the wheels of the mind keep moving with the wheels of fortune. Livy
described Cato Major as having such "body and soul" that no matter where he was born, he would have made himself
fortunate. He had a "versatile mind." If you look closely, you can actually "see" Fortune; she's blind, but she's
not invisible.

Fortune is like the Milky Way—a bunch of small stars that aren't seen separately but give off light together. Your
"luck" is actually a combination of many small, barely noticeable virtues or habits. The Italians noted a weird
one: they say someone who "can't do anything wrong" often has "Poco di matto" (a little bit of madness). Being
a little bit of a fool and not "too honest" are actually the most fortunate traits. People who are "too loyal"
to their country or their boss are never fortunate because they stop focusing on their own path.

A "hasty fortune" (sudden win) makes someone an "enterpriser" or a "remover" (someone who shakes things up). But a
"trained fortune" (gradual success) creates an "able man."

You should honor and respect Fortune, mostly for her "daughters": Confidence and Reputation. Felicity (happiness)
creates both. Wise people often attribute their wins to "Providence" or "Fortune" to avoid the "hater energy"
(envy) of others. Caesar told his pilot in a storm, "You're carrying Caesar and his fortune." Sylla called himself
"Felix" (Lucky) instead of "Magnus" (Great). People who brag too much about their own "wisdom and policy" often end
up unfortunate. Timotheus of Athens kept saying "Fortune had no part in this," and after that, he never prospered again.

Some people's fortunes are like Homer's verses—they just have a natural "slide" and ease that others don't. This
"flow" is often built into the person themselves.
