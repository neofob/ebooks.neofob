# OF PRAISE (THE HYPE MACHINE)

Praise is just a reflection of virtue, but the "mirror" matters. If the praise comes from the "common people," it's
usually fake or mid. They don't really get high-tier virtues; they only praise the basics, or they get "astonished"
by things they don't understand. They're more into "vibes" (species virtutibus similes) than actual substance.

Real fame is like a river—it carries the "light and swollen" things (the fake ones) to the top and drowns the
"weighty and solid" ones. But if people with actual judgment and quality agree that you're great, then your name
is like a high-end perfume—it lasts and fills the room.

Be careful with praise; it's often a trap.
- **The Ordinary Flatterer:** Uses generic compliments that could apply to anyone.
- **The Cunning Flatterer:** Figures out what you already believe about yourself and just echoes it back to you. No
one is as big a flatterer as your own ego.
- **The Impudent Flatterer:** Praises you for the thing you know you're worst at—basically mocking you to your face.

Some praise is just "civility"—like when people tell a king they're great to remind them how they *should*
be. Then there's "malicious praise"—people who praise you just to stir up envy and jealousy from others. This
is the worst kind of enemy.

The move is to use moderate praise, at the right time, and not to a huge crowd. Over-hyping someone or something
just invites people to contradict it and makes them a target for scorn.

Self-praise is generally a fail, unless you're praising your *office* or your *profession*—that's just being
proud of the grind.

The religious leaders (cardinals, friars) often look down on "civil business" (wars, diplomacy, law), calling
it "under-sheriff work." But honestly, those "low-tier" jobs often do more actual good than their high-level
philosophical speculations. St. Paul understood this—he'd call himself a "fool" but would "magnify" his calling.
