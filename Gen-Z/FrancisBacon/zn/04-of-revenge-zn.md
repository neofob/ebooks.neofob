# OF REVENGE (THE TOXIC CYCLE)

Revenge is basically "wild justice," and the more people vibe with it, the more the law needs to delete it. Because
while the first wrong just breaks the law, revenge for that wrong puts the law completely out of a job. Honestly,
when you take revenge, you're just equal to your enemy, but when you let it slide, you're actually superior. It's
a total boss move to pardon; Solomon said it's the literal glory of a man to just pass by an offense. What's gone
is gone and can't be undone, and based people focus on the now and the future; those who obsess over the past are
just trolling themselves.

Nobody does a wrong just for the sake of being wrong—they're usually trying to get a bag, a flex, or some
pleasure. So why be mad at someone for loving themselves more than they love you? And if someone's just toxic for
the sake of it, they're like a thorn or a briar—they prick and scratch because that's literally all they know
how to do. The only tolerable revenge is for the things the law can't fix; but even then, make sure your revenge
isn't something the law *can* punish, otherwise your enemy is still winning and you've just taken a double L.

Some people want the other party to know exactly who sent the revenge. That's the more generous vibe; the joy isn't
just in the hurt, but in making the other person regret their life choices. But the low-key cowards are like an arrow
flying in the dark—they hit and dip. Cosmus, the Duke of Florence, had a savage take on fake friends: "You'll read
that we're told to forgive our enemies, but you'll never read that we're told to forgive our friends." But Job had
a better energy: "Should we take the wins from God and not be chill with the losses too?" The same applies to friends.

One thing's for sure: someone who lives for revenge keeps their own wounds green, preventing them from ever
healing. Public revenges usually hit the mark—like the ones for Caesar or Henry III of France. But private
revenge? Not so much. In fact, vindictive people live like witches—they're mischievous, and they end up with a
tragic ending.
