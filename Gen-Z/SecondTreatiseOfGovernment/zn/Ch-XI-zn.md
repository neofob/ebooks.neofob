# CHAPTER XI

## THE SCOPE OF THE LEGISLATIVES POWER

**Sect. 134.** The main goal of joining a society is to enjoy your property in peace and safety, and the primary
tool for that is the established laws. Therefore, the first and most fundamental rule of any commonwealth is setting
up the legislative power. The first and most basic natural law—which governs even the legislative itself—is
the preservation of the society and, as much as possible, every person in it.

This legislative isn't just the supreme power; it's sacred and unchangeable once the community has placed it
in certain hands. No decree from anyone else, no matter how it's phrased or who backs it, has the force of law
unless it's sanctioned by the legislative that the public chose and appointed. Without this, a law isn't actually
a law because it lacks the one thing it absolutely needs: the consent of the society. Nobody can have the power
to make laws for others without their consent and authority derived from them. Thus, all obedience—even the
most solemn—starts and ends with this supreme power and the laws it makes. No oath to a foreign power or any
lower domestic authority can excuse someone from obeying the legislative when it's acting on trust; nor can any
other power force someone to obey something that contradicts these laws. It would be ridiculous to think you're
ultimately bound to any power in society that isn't the supreme one.

**Sect. 135.** Even though the legislative (whether it's one person or a group, constant or intermittent) is the
supreme power, there are limits:

First, it cannot be absolutely arbitrary over the lives and fortunes of the people. Since the legislative is just
the combined power of every member of society, it can't have more power than those individuals had in the State of
Nature (the OG state). You can't transfer more power than you actually possess; since nobody has absolute arbitrary
power over their own life or the life and property of another, they can't give that power to the government. A
person can't subject themselves to the arbitrary power of another. In the State of Nature, you only had the power
to protect yourself and others according to the law of nature; that's all you give to the commonwealth.

The legislative's power is strictly limited to the public good of the society. Its only goal is preservation,
so it can never have the right to destroy, enslave, or intentionally impoverish its subjects. The obligations
of the law of nature don't stop just because you join a society; they just get more specific and get human laws
attached to them to make sure they're followed. The law of nature is the eternal rule for everyone—legislators
included. The rules they make for others must align with the law of nature (which is the will of God). Since the
fundamental law of nature is the preservation of mankind, no human law can be valid if it goes against that.

**Sect. 136.** Secondly, the supreme authority cannot just rule by random, arbitrary decrees. It is bound to deliver
justice and decide rights using promulgated, standing laws and authorized judges. Because the law of nature is
unwritten and exists only in people's minds, those who misapply it out of passion or interest can't easily be
proven wrong if there's no established judge. This makes it hard to protect property and rights, especially if
everyone is their own judge and executioner in their own case. To fix this, people unite into societies to get the
combined strength of the community to secure and defend their properties using standing rules so everyone knows
what belongs to them. This is why people give up their natural power to the society and trust the legislative to
govern them by clear laws—otherwise, their peace and property would be just as uncertain as they were in the
State of Nature (the OG state).

**Sect. 137.** Absolute arbitrary power—or ruling without settled laws—is totally incompatible with the goals
of society and government. People didn't leave the freedom of the State of Nature only to be tied down by a ruler
who can actually make their lives worse. It can't be supposed that they’d give someone absolute arbitrary power
over their persons and assets. That would be putting themselves in a worse position than the State of Nature,
where they at least had the liberty to defend their rights and were on equal footing with others.

By giving themselves over to an absolute ruler, they've disarmed themselves and armed the ruler, making themselves
prey whenever he feels like it. Being exposed to the arbitrary power of one man who controls 100,000 people is way
worse than being exposed to 100,000 separate people. No one can be sure that a ruler's will is better than anyone
else's, even if his force is 100,000 times stronger. Therefore, no matter the form of government, the ruling power
must govern by declared and accepted laws, not by sudden whims or undocumented resolutions. If the government used
the joint power of the people to force them to obey random, unlimited decrees, they'd be worse off than in the
State of nature. The government's power is purely for the public good, so it must be exercised through established
and public laws—so the people know their duties and are safe, and the rulers are kept within their bounds.

**Sect. 138.** Thirdly, the supreme power cannot take any part of a person's property without their own consent. Since
the whole point of government is the preservation of property, it's a given that people must have property. If
you lose your property just by joining a society, you've lost the very thing you joined to protect—which is a
huge absurdity. People in a society have a right to the goods the law recognizes as theirs, and nobody can take
that substance without their consent. Otherwise, they have no property at all.

It's a mistake to think the legislative can just dispose of subjects' estates arbitrarily. This isn't a huge risk in
governments where the legislative is made of rotating assemblies (whose members eventually become regular subjects
again). But in governments where the legislative is one permanent assembly or one man (like absolute monarchies),
there's a risk they'll start thinking their interests are different from the community's and take whatever they
want. Your property isn't secure if the person who commands the laws can just take your stuff whenever they feel
like it.

**Sect. 139.** No matter who is in charge, government is entrusted with the condition of securing property. The
prince or senate can make laws to regulate property between subjects, but they can never just take the property
for themselves without consent. That would leave the people with nothing. Even "absolute" power isn't arbitrary;
it's still limited by reason and the goals it was created for. Look at military discipline: for the survival of the
army and the commonwealth, soldiers must obey every order, even if it means marching into a cannon or being executed
for deserting. But even a general with the power of life and death can't take a single penny from his soldier's
estate. That blind obedience is necessary for the goal of preservation, but stealing goods has nothing to do with that.

**Sect. 140.** Of course, governments cost money to run, and it's fair that everyone who enjoys protection pays
their share. But this must be done with their own consent (via the majority or their representatives). If someone
claims the power to tax people by their own authority without consent, they are invading the fundamental law of
property and destroying the purpose of government. If someone can just take your stuff whenever they want, you
don't actually have any property.

**Sect. 141.** Fourthly, the legislative cannot transfer the power of making laws to anyone else. Since it's
a delegated power from the people, those who hold it can't pass it on. Only the people can decide the form of
government and who makes the laws. Once the people say, "We'll be governed by these men in this form," nobody else
can decide who makes the laws for them. The legislative power was granted by a voluntary agreement specifically
to *make laws*, not to *make legislators*. Therefore, the legislative cannot transfer its authority to others.

**Sect. 142.** These are the boundaries set by the trust of the society and the laws of God and nature for every
commonwealth:

1. They must govern by public, established laws that apply to everyone—rich or poor, court favorite or farmer.
2. These laws must be designed for the ultimate good of the people.
3. They cannot tax the people without the consent of the people (through themselves or deputies). This mostly
applies to governments with a permanent legislative.
4. The legislative cannot transfer the power of law-making to anyone else.
