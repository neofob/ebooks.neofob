# CHAPTER VI

## OF PATERNAL POWER

**Sect. 52.** It might seem like an annoying nitpick to complain about the words and names the world uses in a
discourse like this. But it's probably worth offering new terms when the old ones lead people into mistakes. The
term "paternal power" is a good example; it makes it sound like the power over children belongs entirely to the
father, as if the mother has no share. If we look at reason or revelation, we'll see she has an equal title. Maybe
we should call it "parental power" instead? Whatever obligation nature and birth place on children, it must apply
equally to both causes of that birth. The laws of God also join them together without distinction: "Honour thy
father and thy mother" (Exod. xx. 12), "Whosoever curseth his father or his mother" (Lev. xx. 9), "Ye shall fear
every man his mother and his father" (Lev. xix. 3), "Children, obey your parents" (Eph. vi. 1). That's the vibe
of both the Old and New Testaments.

**Sect. 53.** If people had just considered this one thing, they might have avoided the huge mistakes they've
made about the power of parents. Even if this power were called "absolute dominion" or "regal authority," it
would still sound absurd if it were called *parental* power, because that would imply it belongs to the mother
too. Those who fight so hard for the "absolute power of fatherhood" would be in a tough spot if the mother had a
share in it. Their version of monarchy would also fail, because the fundamental authority they use to justify a
single ruler's government would actually be split between two people. But let's move past the naming issue.

**Sect. 54.** Although I said in Chapter II that all men are naturally equal, I don't mean every *kind* of
equality. Age or virtue can give people a just precedence. Talent and merit can put some above the average. Birth,
alliances, or benefits might make some people owe respect to others due to nature, gratitude, or other factors. But
all this is compatible with the equality that all humans share regarding jurisdiction or dominion over one
another. That's the equality I was talking about: the equal right every person has to their natural freedom,
without being subject to the will or authority of another.

**Sect. 55.** I admit that children aren't born in this full state of equality, though they are born *to* it. Their
parents have a kind of rule and jurisdiction over them when they enter the world and for a while after; but it's
only temporary. The bonds of this subjection are like the swaddling clothes they're wrapped in as infants for
support. As they grow and gain reason, those bonds loosen until they eventually drop off, leaving the person in
control of their own life.

**Sect. 56.** Adam was created as a perfect man—his body and mind were already at full strength and reason. He
was capable from the first second of existing to provide for his own support and govern his actions according to
the law of reason God had implanted in him. His descendants, however, are all born as infants: weak, helpless, and
without knowledge. To fill the gaps of this imperfect state until they grow up, Adam and Eve (and all parents after
them) were obligated by the law of nature to preserve, nourish, and educate their children. They didn't do this as
"owners" of their children's work, but as caretakers of the Almighty's work, for whom they would be accountable.

**Sect. 57.** The law that governed Adam was the same one for all his descendants: the law of reason. But his
offspring entered the world differently—through natural birth, which left them ignorant and without the use of
reason. Therefore, they weren't immediately under that law. You can't be under a law that hasn't been announced
to you. Since this law is announced only through reason, someone who hasn't reached the use of reason isn't under
it. Adam's children weren't free immediately upon birth.

In its true sense, law isn't about limiting freedom, but about directing a free and intelligent agent toward their
own best interest, and it only prescribes what is for the general good. If people could be happier without it,
the law would be useless and vanish. It's not "confinement" to be fenced off from bogs and cliffs. The goal of
law is not to abolish or restrain, but to preserve and enlarge freedom. In any state of beings capable of laws,
where there is no law, there is no freedom. Liberty is being free from restraint and violence from others—which
is impossible without law.

Freedom isn't just the liberty for everyone to do whatever they want (because then everyone would just be dominated
by someone else's mood). Instead, it's the liberty to dispose of and order your own person, actions, possessions,
and total assets (Property) within the laws you're under, and not being subject to the arbitrary will of another. In
nature, this means being under no other restraint but the law of nature.

**Sect. 58.** The power parents have over their children comes from the duty to take care of them during the
imperfect state of childhood. Parents are obligated to inform their minds and govern their actions until reason
takes over. God gave humans an understanding to direct their actions, and with that, a freedom of will and liberty of
acting—as long as they stay within the law. But while a child doesn't have the understanding to direct their own
will, they can't have a will of their own to follow. The person who understands for them must also will for them;
they must set the rules and regulate their actions. But once the child reaches the state that made the father a
freeman, the son is a freeman too.

**Sect. 59.** This applies to all laws, natural or civil. What made a man free under the law of nature? A state
of maturity where he's capable of knowing the law, so he can keep his actions within its bounds. Once he reaches
that state, he's presumed to know how the law guides him and how to use his freedom. Until then, someone else must
guide him. If that state of reason makes the father free, it makes the son free too.

Is a man under the law of England? What made him free under that law—meaning, the liberty to handle his actions
and possessions as he wants, within the law's permission? A capacity for knowing that law, which the law itself
assumes happens at age 21 (or sooner in some cases). If this made the father free, it makes the son free too. Until
then, the law says the son has no will of his own and must be guided by the will of his father or guardian. If
the father dies without appointing a substitute, the law ensures a tutor is provided to govern the son until he
reaches maturity. After that, father and son are equally free—just as tutor and pupil are after the student's
minority. They are both subjects of the same law, and the father has no dominion over the life, liberty, or assets
of his son, whether they are just under the law of nature or under a formal government.

**Sect. 60.** But if some natural defect prevents someone from ever reaching the level of reason where they can know
the law and even live by its rules, they are never capable of being a free man. They are never "let loose" to follow
their own will (because they have no understanding to guide it) and remain under the guidance and government of others
for their entire life. This is why lunatics and "idiots" are never set free from the government of their parents.

As Hooker says in *Eccl. Pol. lib. i. sec. 7*, children who haven't reached the age of reason, and those excluded
by natural defects, and those who currently lack the use of reason, are guided by the reason of others—tutors
who seek and procure their good for them. All this is just the duty that God and nature placed on humans (and other
creatures) to preserve their offspring until they can take care of themselves. This hardly proves that parents have
"regal authority."

**Sect. 61.** We are born free because we are born rational, even if we can't actually exercise either yet. Age brings
both. This shows how natural freedom and subjection to parents can coexist; they're based on the same principle. A
child is free "by his father's title"—by his father's understanding, which governs him until he has his own.

The freedom of an adult and the subjection of a child are so consistent and distinct that even the most blinded
proponents of monarchy-by-fatherhood can't miss the difference. If their doctrine were true—if Adam's right
heir were known and sat on a throne with absolute unlimited power—and he died the moment his heir was born,
wouldn't that baby (despite being a sovereign) still be subject to his mother, nurse, tutors, and governors until
he grew up? His survival, health, and education would require him to be directed by others. Nobody would think
this restraint spoils his liberty or sovereignty or gives his empire away to his tutors. This government just’s
preparing him to rule. If you ask me when my son is old enough to be free, I'll say: "Exactly when his monarch is
old enough to govern." As for when someone has reached the use of reason enough to be bound by laws, that's easier
for common sense to discern than for any "expert" to determine.

**Sect. 62.** Governments themselves recognize that there's a time when people start acting like free men. That's
why they don't require oaths of allegiance or public submission until that age.

**Sect. 63.** Human freedom and the liberty to act according to one's will are grounded in having reason, which
can teach the laws one must follow and show the limits of that freedom. Turning someone loose with unrestrained
liberty before they have reason isn't giving them their natural right to be free; it's throwing them to the brutes
and abandoning them to a wretched state below that of a human. This is why parents have the authority to govern
their children's minority. God made it their job to care for their offspring and gave them the natural tenderness
and concern to temper this power and use it for the children's good.

**Sect. 64.** But there's no reason this care should turn into absolute arbitrary dominion. A father's power only
extends to the discipline needed to give his children strength, health, and a sound mind, making them useful to
themselves and others—and if necessary, making them work for their own survival once they're able. The mother
has an equal share in this power.

**Sect. 65.** In fact, this power doesn't really belong to the father by any special natural right, but only
because he's the guardian of his children. When he stops caring for them, he loses his power over them. This power
is tied to their nourishment and education. A foster-father of an abandoned child has as much power as a biological
father. The bare act of procreating doesn't give a man power over his kids if he stops caring for them there.

Think about the parts of the world where a woman has more than one husband, or in some parts of America where, if a
husband and wife part, the children stay with the mother and are entirely under her care. If the father dies while
the children are young, don't they owe the same obedience to their mother as they would have to their father? Also,
does a mother have "legislative power" over her children? Can she make permanent rules for their assets and liberties
for the rest of their lives? Or can she execute them for breaking rules? No. That's the power of a magistrate, which
a father doesn't even have a shadow of. His command is temporary and doesn't reach their life or assets. It's just
a help for the imperfection of their childhood. A father can't dispose of the goods his children earned themselves
or received as gifts, nor can he control their liberty once they reach the age of discretion. At that point, the
father's "empire" ends; he can't dispose of his son's liberty any more than he could any other man's. And he's
not some absolute jurisdiction; a man has divine permission to "leave father and mother and cleave to his wife."

**Sect. 66.** Even when a child becomes as free as their father, they still owe their parents honor based on the laws
of God and nature. God used parents as instruments to continue the human race and give life to their children. Since
parents were obligated to nourish and bring up their offspring, children have a perpetual obligation to honor their
parents. This means an internal esteem and reverence shown through outward expressions, avoiding anything that
would hurt or insult the parents, and helping them in times of need. No amount of freedom or state-hood absolves
children from this.

But this is very different from giving parents a power of command or a legislative authority over their children's
lives or liberties. Owing honor, respect, and gratitude is one thing; requiring absolute obedience and submission
is another. Even a monarch on his throne owes his mother honor, but that doesn't make him subject to her government.

**Sect. 67.** The subjection of a minor is a temporary government that ends when the child reaches maturity. The
honor due to parents is a perpetual right to respect and support that lasts a lifetime, scaled to the care and
kindness the parent provided. Confusing these two—the right of tuition during minority and the right of honor
for life—is where most of the mistakes happen.

Actually, the first one (education) is more of a privilege for the children and a duty for the parents than a
"power" of the parents. Nourishing and educating children is a charge parents must fulfill for the children's own
good. While this involves commanding and chastising, God built a natural tenderness into parents so they rarely
use too much rigor. God showed this when He told the Israelites that He chastened them as a man chastens his son
(Deut. viii. 5)—with affection and only as much discipline as was absolutely necessary for their good. This is
the power children are told to obey, so that their parents' pain and care aren't wasted.

**Sect. 68.** On the other hand, honor and support—whatever gratitude requires giving back for the benefits
received—is the indispensable duty of the child and the privilege of the parents. This is for the parents' advantage,
while the other was for the child's. Education seems more powerful because the ignorance and weakness of childhood
require visible rule and correction. But the duty of "honor" requires less obedience, even though the obligation
is stronger as the child grows. A man who has children of his own isn't expected to submit to his father's every
whim just because the commandment says "Children obey your parents," especially if the father treats him like a boy.

**Sect. 69.** The first part of paternal power (education) ends at a certain age; it's a job that finishes. It
can even be transferred to others—like when a man makes his son an apprentice. But the duty of honor remains
eternal. Nothing cancels that. The father's authority doesn't take away the mother's right to be honored.

None of this gives parents the power to make laws or use penalties that affect a child's estate, liberty, or
life. The power to command ends with minority. After that, while honor, respect, and gratitude are always due, the
father has no "scepter"—no sovereign power of command. He has no dominion over his son's property or actions. He
can't dictate his son's will in everything, though the son might choose to defer to him as a courtesy.

**Sect. 70.** You can owe honor to an old or wise man, defense to a friend, relief to the distressed, and gratitude
to a benefactor—even to a point where you can't possibly pay it all back. But none of that gives those people
the right to make laws for you. These obligations aren't just based on the title of "father"—they're also owed
to the mother, and the level of requirement varies based on the actual care and kindness provided to each child.

**Sect. 71.** This explains how parents in societies (where they are subjects themselves) still have power over
their children. If political power and paternal power were the same thing, then by the time the prince (the ultimate
father) got all the power, the subjects wouldn't have any paternal power left. But these two powers are completely
separate, built on different foundations, and serve different goals. Every subject who is a father has as much
paternal power over his kids as the prince has over his. And every prince who has parents owes them the same filial
duty that the lowest subject owes theirs. The prince doesn't have some special "paternal" dominion over his subjects.

**Sect. 72.** While the obligation to raise children and the obligation to honor parents cover most of
this relationship, there's one other power fathers usually have: the power to decide how to distribute their
estate. Because the children usually expect an inheritance according to the laws and customs of the country,
the father can use this—being more or less liberal—as a way to encourage the child to follow his wishes.

**Sect. 73.** This is a strong tie for obedience. Also, since enjoying land requires submission to the government
of the country where the land is, some people assume a father can obligate his kids to a specific government
just because he was a subject. But that's not a natural tie; it's a voluntary submission. Every person's child is
naturally as free as they are and can choose which society to join. But if they want the inheritance, they have
to take it on the same terms their ancestors did and submit to the conditions attached.

Fathers use the inheritance as a reward for obedience, even after the kids are adults. This isn't a special "right
of fatherhood," but just the same power a Frenchman would have over an Englishman if he promised to leave him an
estate. If the Englishman wants the land, he has to follow the laws of France.

**Sect. 74.** To wrap up: the father's power to command only lasts through childhood and is only intended for the
discipline of that age. The honor and respect (the "piety" the Latins talked about) that parents are owed for a
lifetime doesn't give the father any power to make laws or punish his children. He has no dominion over his son's
property or actions.

It's easy to see how, in the early days of the world (or in unpopulated areas), the father of a family could become
its prince. He had been the ruler since the kids were babies. Since they needed some government to live together,
the kids—once adults—probably just tacitly agreed to keep the father in charge, as it felt like a natural
continuation. This just meant they allowed the father to exercise the "executive power of the law of nature" (which
every free man has) in the family. They essentially gave him monarchical power as long as they stayed in the family.

But this wasn't based on "paternal right." We know this because if a stranger came into the family and killed
one of the children, the father could punish or kill that stranger—not because of paternal authority (since the
stranger isn't his child), but because of the executive power of the law of nature. He was the only one who could
do it in the family because the children had stepped back to let his authority lead.

(Footnote: It's not a wild theory to say that the head of every household was basically a king. When households
joined into civil societies, kings were the first governors. This is probably why the term "father" stayed with
them. It's also why early kings acted as priests, since fathers did that first. But this isn't the only type of
government. The downsides of one kind led to others. All public government came from deliberate talk and agreement
between men who found it convenient. Nature doesn't require government; humans could have lived without it, as
some have.)

**Sect. 75.** It was easy and almost natural for children to give their tacit consent to the father's authority. They
were used to following his direction and letting him settle their disputes since childhood. When they became men,
who better to rule them? Their possessions were small and didn't cause big fights, and when they did, who was
a better umpire than the one who raised them and loved them? They didn't feel the need to distinguish between
minority and adulthood, or care about a specific age (like 21) to become "free," because they were happy under
their father's protection. They found more security in their father's rule than anywhere else.

**Sect. 76.** This is how the natural fathers of families slowly became political monarchs. Depending on how long
they lived and who their heirs were, this laid the foundation for hereditary or elective kingdoms. If princes'
titles come from their "fathers' right," and that's proof that fathers have a natural right to political authority
just because they happened to be the ones ruling, then that same logic would prove that all princes should also
be priests—since the father was the priest of the household as much as he was the ruler.
