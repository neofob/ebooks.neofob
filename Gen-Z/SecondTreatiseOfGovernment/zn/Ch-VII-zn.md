# CHAPTER VII

## OF POLITICAL OR CIVIL SOCIETY

**Sect. 77.** God made humans in a way that, in His own judgment, it wasn't a vibe for us to be alone. He put
us under strong pressures of necessity, convenience, and just a natural pull to form societies, and He gave us
the understanding and language to keep those societies going and actually enjoy them. The first society ever was
between husband and wife, which then started the bond between parents and children; eventually, the relationship
between master and servant was added to the mix. While all these might overlap and form one single family—where
the head of the house has a certain kind of rule over the home—none of these, alone or together, qualify as a
"political society." We'll see why when we look at the different goals, ties, and boundaries of each.

**Sect. 78.** Conjugal society (marriage) starts with a voluntary compact between a man and a woman. While it's
mostly about a shared communion and right to each other's bodies for the purpose of procreation, it also brings
mutual support, assistance, and a shared interest. This isn't just to unite their own care and affection, but is also
necessary for their offspring, who have a right to be nourished and maintained until they can provide for themselves.

**Sect. 79.** Since the goal of the union between male and female isn't just making babies but continuing the species,
this union should last as long as it takes for the young ones to be supported by those who created them, until they
can shift for themselves. The infinitely wise Creator set this rule for all His creations. In grass-eating animals,
the union lasts only as long as the act of mating; since the mother's milk is enough to nourish the young until
they can eat grass, the male just begets and doesn't really worry about the female or the kids. But in predators,
the union lasts longer. Because the mother can't easily support herself and her many offspring on her own—since
hunting is a much harder and more dangerous way to live than eating grass—the father's help is necessary to
maintain the family until the young can hunt for themselves. We see the same in birds (except some domestic ones
where plenty of food means the rooster doesn't have to help); the parents stay mates until the young can fly and
provide for themselves.

**Sect. 80.** This is the main reason why humans stay together for a longer union than other animals: because the
female can conceive and often has another child long before the previous one is independent. The father, who is
obligated to care for his children, must stay in the conjugal society longer than other creatures, whose young are
independent before the next mating season. We can really admire the wisdom of the Creator here: by giving humans
foresight and the ability to save for the future, He made it necessary for the union of man and wife to be more
lasting. This encourages industry and better unites their interests to provide for their kids; if the unions were
just temporary and fragile, it would totally mess up that process.

**Sect. 81.** Even though these ties make human marriage more firm and lasting than in other species, it raises a
question: why can't this compact—where procreation, education, and inheritance are handled—be made terminable? Why
can't it end by consent, at a certain time, or under certain conditions, like any other voluntary contract? There's
no natural necessity or reason why it must always be for life (at least for those not bound by a specific law that
says it must be perpetual).

**Sect. 82.** Since husbands and wives have one common interest but different perspectives, they'll naturally have
different wills sometimes. It's therefore necessary that the final decision—the "rule"—be placed somewhere. It
naturally falls to the man, as the stronger and more capable. But this only applies to things of their common
interest and property; the wife keeps full and free possession of whatever is her own by contract. The husband
has no more power over her life than she has over his. He is far from being an absolute monarch; in many cases,
the wife has the liberty to separate from him if natural right or their contract allows it (whether that contract
was made by them in the State of Nature or by the laws of the country they live in). The children then go to the
father or mother as the contract determines.

**Sect. 83.** Since all the goals of marriage can be achieved under a political government just as well as in
the State of Nature, the civil magistrate doesn't limit the natural rights or powers necessary for those goals
(like procreation and mutual support). The magistrate only settles controversies that might arise. If absolute
sovereignty and power over life and death naturally belonged to the husband, marriage couldn't exist in any
country where the husband isn't allowed that absolute authority. But since marriage doesn't require that kind of
power, it's not built into the state of conjugal society. Conjugal society could totally work without it. In fact,
things like shared goods, maintenance, and other details can be regulated by the contract that unites the couple,
as long as it doesn't stop the procreation and raising of children. Nothing is necessary for a society that isn't
necessary for the goals that society was made for.

**Sect. 84.** I've already talked a lot about the society between parents and children and their respective
rights in the previous chapter, so I don't need to repeat it here. But it's clear that it's very different from
a political society.

**Sect. 85.** "Master" and "servant" are old terms, but they apply to very different conditions. A freeman becomes
a servant by selling his service to another for a set time in exchange for wages. Even if this puts him into the
family and under the usual household discipline, the master only has temporary power—and only as much as is
written in the contract. But there's another kind of servant we call "slaves." These are captives taken in a just
war, who are subject to the absolute dominion and arbitrary power of their masters by the right of nature. These
people have forfeited their lives, their liberties, and their estates. Being in a state of slavery, they can't own
property and therefore aren't part of a civil society (since the main goal of civil society is to preserve property).

**Sect. 86.** Let's look at a head of a family with all these subordinates—wife, children, servants, and slaves—all
under the domestic rule of one house. No matter how much this looks like a little commonwealth in terms of order
or size, it's very different in its constitution, power, and goals. If you want to call it a monarchy and the
*paterfamilias* an absolute monarch, it's a very weak and short-lived power. As we've seen, the master of a family
has very different and limited power over the different people in it. Except for the slave (and the family is still
a family with or without slaves), he has no legislative power over their life or death—no more than a mistress
of the family would. He certainly can't have absolute power over the whole family if he only has limited power
over each individual in it. So, to see how a family differs from a truly political society, we just need to look
at what a political society actually is.

**Sect. 87.** Since humans are born with a title to perfect freedom and an equal right to enjoy all the privileges
of the law of nature, every person has the natural power not only to protect their own property (life, liberty,
and estate) but to judge and punish those who break the law, even with death if the crime is heinous enough. But
no political society can exist without the power to preserve property and punish offenders. Therefore, a political
society only exists where every member has given up this natural power and handed it over to the community (except
in cases where they can appeal to the law for protection).

By excluding private judgment, the community becomes the umpire. It uses settled, indifferent rules that are the
same for everyone, and uses authorized officials to execute those rules and settle differences. This is how you
tell who is actually in a political society: those who are united in one body and have a common law and court
system to appeal to for deciding controversies and punishing offenders are in a civil society. Those who have no
such common appeal (on earth) are still in the State of Nature—each acting as their own judge and executioner.

**Sect. 88.** This is how a commonwealth gets the power to set punishments for different crimes (the power to make
laws) and the power to punish injuries done by outsiders (the power of war and peace)—all for the preservation
of the property of all members. While every member of a civil society gives up their personal power to punish
based on private judgment, they give the commonwealth the right to use their force to execute the judgments
of the legislative. These are effectively their own judgments, since they were made by themselves or their
representatives. This is the origin of the legislative and executive powers: judging by standing laws how to
punish internal crimes, and making occasional judgments on how to handle external injuries, while employing the
full force of the community when needed.

**Sect. 89.** Wherever any group of people unite into one society and give up their individual executive power
of the law of nature to the public, that's where you have a political or civil society. This happens whenever
people in the State of Nature agree to make one people, one body politic, under one supreme government; or when
someone joins an existing government. By doing this, they authorize the society (the legislative) to make laws
for the public good, and they owe their own assistance to enforce those laws. This moves people from a state of
nature into a commonwealth by setting up a judge on earth with the authority to settle all controversies and fix
injuries. If there's no such decisive power to appeal to, people are still in the state of nature.

**Sect. 90.** This means that absolute monarchy—which some people think is the only government in the world—is
actually inconsistent with civil society. It can't be a form of civil government at all. The whole point of civil
society is to avoid the problems of the State of Nature (where everyone is a judge in their own case) by setting
up a known authority that everyone can appeal to and obey. Wherever people don't have such an authority to settle
differences, they are still in the state of nature. This includes everyone under an absolute prince.

**Sect. 91.** Since an absolute prince has both legislative and executive power in himself, there's no independent
judge to appeal to for a fair and indifferent decision regarding injuries caused by the prince or his orders. Whether
he's called a Czar, a Grand Seignior, or whatever, he's just as much in the state of nature as anyone else. Wherever
two people have no standing rule and no common judge on earth to settle a fight, they are in the state of nature. The
only difference is that the subject of an absolute prince is worse off: in the normal state of nature, he can at
least judge his own right and try to defend it; under an absolute monarch, he's denied that liberty and is exposed
to all the misery of someone who is in a state of nature but also corrupted by flattery and armed with power.

**Sect. 92.** Anyone who thinks absolute power "purifies" people or fixes the baseness of human nature just
needs to look at history. Someone who would be a jerk in the woods of America would probably be even worse on a
throne, where they can use "learning and religion" to justify their actions and use the sword to silence anyone
who questions them. If you want to see what happens when absolute monarchy reaches "perfection," just look at the
rest of the story about Ceylon.

**Sect. 93.** In absolute monarchies, subjects do have laws and judges to settle disputes between themselves. Everyone
agrees that's necessary, and anyone who wants to take that away is an enemy of society. But we should doubt if
this comes from a real love of mankind. It's just what any person who loves their own power and profit would do:
keep their "animals" from hurting each other so they can keep working for the master's benefit.

If you ask what security you have against the violence of the absolute ruler himself, you're told that just asking
the question is a crime punishable by death. Between subjects, there are laws and judges for peace; but the ruler
is "above it all" because he has the power to do the most harm. To ask for protection from the strongest hand is
called "faction and rebellion." It's as if people left the state of nature to enter a society where everyone but
one person is restrained by law, while that one person keeps all the liberties of the state of nature, plus more
power and total impunity. That would be like avoiding a fox but choosing to be eaten by a lion.

**Sect. 94.** No matter what flatterers say, people can still feel pain. When they realize that some person—no
matter their status—is outside the bounds of civil society and that they have no appeal against them, they
realize they are in the state of nature relative to that person. They'll do whatever they can to get the safety
and security that civil society was actually made for.

Maybe at first, some excellent man got the respect of others and they let him handle their disputes because he
was honest and wise. But when time passed and successors weren't as great, people realized their property wasn't
secure. Since the only goal of government is the preservation of property, they couldn't be at rest until the
legislative power was placed in collective bodies—like a senate or parliament. This way, every single person
is subject to the laws, even the rulers. No one can avoid the law by claiming superiority. In a civil society,
no one is exempt from the law. If someone can do whatever they want and there's no appeal on earth for redress,
they are perfectly still in the state of nature.

**Sect. 95.** Men are naturally free and equal, so nobody can be forced into political power without their own
consent. The only way to leave natural liberty and join a civil society is to agree with other people to unite into
a community for a safer, more peaceful life and the secure enjoyment of their properties. Any number of people
can do this without hurting the freedom of others (who remain in the state of nature). Once a group consents to
make a government, they become one body politic, and the majority has the right to act and make decisions for the rest.

**Sect. 96.** When individuals consent to form a community, they create a single body that acts as one, and that
movement is directed by the will of the majority. Since the community is based on the consent of the individuals,
and a single body must move in one direction, it has to follow the greater force (the majority). Otherwise, the
community couldn't act or stay together. This is why in assemblies, the act of the majority is the act of the whole.

**Sect. 97.** By agreeing to join a body politic, every person obligates themselves to submit to the majority. If
they were still free to ignore any decree they didn't personally like, the "compact" would mean nothing. It would
be the same as the liberty they had in the state of nature.

**Sect. 98.** If the majority's decision isn't accepted as the act of the whole, then only a 100% unanimous vote
could make a decision. But that's impossible in any sizeable group due to illness, business, different opinions,
and conflicting interests. A society based on total unanimity would dissolve the moment it was born. No rational
creature would start a society just to have it instantly fall apart.

**Sect. 99.** Anyone moving from the state of nature into a community must be understood to give up all the power
necessary to achieve their goals to the majority. This is done simply by agreeing to unite into one political
society. That's the only "compact" needed. The start of any lawful government in the world is simply the consent
of free people to unite.

**Sect. 100.** There are two common objections to this. First, that history doesn't show groups of equal, independent
men meeting and starting a government this way. Second, that it's impossible because everyone is born under some
government and must submit to it.

**Sect. 101.** To the first: it's not surprising that history doesn't mention people in the state of nature much. The
hardships of that life and the desire for society mean that as soon as people got together, they united. If we can't
assume people were ever in the state of nature because there aren't many records, we might as well assume the great
armies of ancient kings were never children, since we only hear about them as adults. Government usually precedes
records. Most people only start looking into their origins after their society has become stable and safe. Most
early polities are either clear examples of voluntary union or show strong evidence of it.

**Sect. 102.** It's a weird move to deny facts just because they don't fit your theory. The beginnings of Rome and
Venice were formed by the union of free, independent people with no natural superiority. Josephus Acosta also noted
that in many parts of America, there was no formal government; people lived in troops and chose their captains
as needed for peace or war. Even if they were subject to their fathers, that didn't stop them from uniting into
a political society. These societies all began from voluntary union and mutual agreement.

**Sect. 103.** I'm sure those who left Sparta with Palantus would be considered free men who set up their own
government by consent. I've given plenty of historical examples. If the lack of records is an argument against this,
then the people arguing for "paternal empire" should probably just give up, because they probably can't find many
historical examples of that either. In fact, if they looked too closely at how most governments actually started,
they'd find very little to support their design.

**Sect. 104.** With reason on our side and historical examples showing that peaceful governments started with the
consent of the people, there's little room for doubt about how lawful governments are erected. (I'll talk about
conquest later).

**Sect. 105.** I won't deny that if we look back, most early commonwealths were led by one man. In small,
isolated families, government often started with the father. Since the father already had the natural power to
punish offenders, the children (even adults) were likely to submit to him. He was the most trusted person, and
the habit of obeying him since childhood made it easy. If they needed a ruler, the father was the most obvious
choice—unless he was cruel or unfit. When the father died and the heir was unfit, or when multiple families
joined, they used their natural freedom to pick the ablest person to rule them. This is seen in the Americas,
where they generally prefer the heir of a king but will pass him over for the bravest man if the heir is weak.

**Sect. H06.** Early governments were often in one hand, but that doesn't mean government is naturally
monarchical. People probably chose one ruler at first because they were used to their father's rule from childhood,
and they found a single, competent leader efficient for their needs. Because they hadn't experienced tyranny
and lived simple lives with few property disputes, they didn't think to create checks and balances or split the
power. They just needed defense against foreign invasions.

**Sect. 108.** The kings of the ancient Americas are a great example. Since there wasn't much money or competition
for land, their kings were basically just generals. They had absolute power in war, but at home, the decisions
were made by the people or a council.

**Sect. 109.** In early Israel, the judges and first kings were mainly army captains. Jephtha was made head and
captain to fight the Ammonites. Gideon was a judge because he fought the Midianites. Saul was anointed to be a
captain to save Israel from the Philistines. Even when David took over, the focus was on his ability to lead the
army. When the tribes submitted to David, they reminded him that he had already been their general under Saul. In
those days, "king" basically meant "general."

**Sect. 110.** Whether a family grew into a commonwealth (father $\rightarrow$ son) or separate families joined
together to pick a leader, the result was often a single ruler. This happened because people trusted their fathers,
or they needed a strong general for protection. In the early stages, these rulers were trusted to act for the
public good. Without "nursing fathers" to take care of them, early societies would have collapsed.

**Sect. 111.** In the "golden age," people had more virtue and better governors, and there was no fighting over
"prerogative" or "privilege." But as vanity, luxury, and ambition grew in later ages, princes started having
interests separate from their people. This made it necessary to examine the original rights of government and find
ways to restrain the abuses of power that people had entrusted to others for their own good.

**Sect. 112.** So, people who were naturally free used their consent to put rule in one man's hand, usually because
it felt safe and efficient. They didn't believe in "divine right"—that's a recent invention. They just used
their reason. All peaceful governments started with the consent of the people.

**Sect. 113.** The other objection is that everyone is born under some government and therefore can't be free to
start a new one. If that were true, how did any lawful monarchy ever start? If one person born under a ruler is
free to start a new empire, then everyone born under a ruler is equally free to do so. Otherwise, there's only
one lawful government in the world, and we're just waiting for them to tell us which one it is.

**Sect. 114.** Every person is born a subject to a father or prince, but that doesn't mean they are under a
perpetual tie of subjection without their own consent. History is full of examples of people withdrawing their
obedience from the jurisdiction they were born under to start new governments. This is how so many small kingdoms
were formed before the stronger ones swallowed them. If paternal sovereignty were a natural right, there would
only be one universal monarchy.

**Sect. 116.** Being born into an ancient polity with established laws doesn't make you a natural subject any more
than being born in the woods does. Some argue that because our ancestors gave up their liberty, we are bound to the
same government. While a person is bound by the promises they make for themselves, they cannot bind their children. A
son, once an adult, is as free as his father. A father can attach conditions to the land he leaves his son, which
might oblige the son to stay in that community if he wants the property, but that's different from a natural tie.

**Sect. 117.** Because most governments don't let their territory be broken up, a son usually has to accept the
same terms as his father to keep the land. This makes them members of the society. Since this happens individually
as each person reaches adulthood, people often mistake it for a natural subjection.

**S. 118.** Governments themselves know this. They don't claim power over a son just because they had power over
the father. For example, if an Englishman and English woman have a child in France, the child is not automatically
a subject of England (he needs to be admitted) nor a subject of France (the father can bring him away). A child
is born a subject of no country. They are under their father's care until the age of discretion, and then they
are free to choose which body politic to join. This proves there's no ancestral compact that binds a person.

**Sect. 119.** Since humans are naturally free and only subject to power through consent, we need to define what
"consent" means. There's "express consent" (saying it clearly), which makes someone a full member of a society. But
there's also "tacit consent." I argue that anyone who enjoys any part of a government's territory—whether they
own land, rent a room for a week, or just walk on the highway—gives their tacit consent to obey the laws while
they are there. Simply being within the territory is enough.

**Sect. 120.** When someone first joins a commonwealth, they also submit their possessions to that community. It
would be a contradiction to join a society to regulate property but then claim your own land is exempt from the
society's laws. So, by joining the society, you unite your person and your possessions to it. Anyone who later
enjoys that land must accept the conditions it comes with: submitting to the government.

**Sect. 121.** But since the government's jurisdiction over a non-member only reaches them as long as they enjoy
the land, this obligation ends when the enjoyment ends. A person who only gave tacit consent is free to leave
and join another society or start a new one in an unpossessed area. But someone who gave *express* consent is
permanently bound to that commonwealth unless the government dissolves or they are officially cut off from it.

**Sect. 122.** Just living in a country and enjoying its protection doesn't make you a "member" of that society
in the permanent sense. It's like staying in someone's family for a while; you follow the house rules, but you
aren't family. Foreigners are bound to obey the laws while they are there, but they don't become subjects unless
they make a positive, express engagement and compact to join.
