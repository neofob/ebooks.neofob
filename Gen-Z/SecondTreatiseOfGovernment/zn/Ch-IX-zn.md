# CHAPTER IX

## THE GOALS OF POLITICAL SOCIETY AND GOVERNMENT

**Sect. 123.** If humans in the State of Nature (the OG state) are as free as I've said—absolute lords of
their own person and assets, equal to the greatest, and subject to nobody—why would they ever give up that
freedom? Why subject themselves to some other power? The obvious answer is that while they have that right in
the State of Nature, actually enjoying it is super uncertain and constantly exposed to the vibes (and attacks)
of others. Since everyone is a "king" and equal, and most people aren't strictly following equity and justice,
enjoying your property in this state is very unsafe and insecure. This makes people willing to leave a condition
that, however free, is full of fear and constant danger. They seek out and join societies—either existing ones
or new ones—for the mutual preservation of their lives, liberties, and estates, which I'll call by the general
name: Property (the total assets: life, liberty, and estate).

**Sect. 124.** The big, main goal of people uniting into commonwealths and putting themselves under government is
the preservation of their property. There are a few things missing in the State of Nature that make this hard.

First, there's no established, settled, known law that everyone agrees on as the standard for right and wrong and
the common measure for settling fights. Even though the law of nature is clear and intelligible to any rational
person, people are biased by their own interests (and often ignorant because they don't study it), so they don't
usually accept it as a binding law when it applies to their own specific cases.

**Sect. 125.** Secondly, in the State of Nature, there's no known, indifferent judge with the authority to settle
differences according to the established law. Since every person in that state is both the judge and the executioner
of the law of nature, people are biased toward themselves. Passion and revenge often lead them too far and too
heatedly in their own cases, while negligence and lack of interest make them too lax in other people's cases.

**Sect. 126.** Thirdly, in the State of Nature, there's often no power to actually enforce a correct sentence. People
who commit injustices will rarely just accept the punishment if they are strong enough to fight back by force. This
makes punishing them dangerous and frequently destructive for whoever tries to do it.

**Sect. 127.** So, despite all the privileges of the State of Nature (the OG state), people are actually in a bad
spot while they're in it, so they're quickly driven into society. The inconveniences of everyone exercising their
own power to punish others in an irregular and uncertain way make them seek sanctuary under the established laws
of government to preserve their property. This is why they're so willing to give up their individual power to
punish and let it be exercised only by those appointed by the community, using rules that the community (or the
people they authorize) agrees on. This is the origin of both the legislative and executive power, as well as the
governments and societies themselves.

**Sect. 128.** In the State of Nature, besides the liberty of innocent delights, a person has two powers.

The first is the power to do whatever they think is necessary for the preservation of themselves and others, as long
as it's allowed by the law of nature. By this law, all humans are one community and one society, distinct from all
other creatures. If humans weren't corrupted and degenerate, there would be no need for any other kind of society;
there would be no reason to separate from this great natural community and form smaller, separate associations.

The other power is the power to punish the crimes committed against that law. A person gives up both of these
when they join a private (or "particular") political society and incorporate into a commonwealth, separate from
the rest of mankind.

**Sect. 129.** The first power—doing whatever they thought was best for the preservation of themselves and
others—is given up to be regulated by the laws made by the society, as far as the preservation of the society
requires. These laws often limit the liberty the person had under the law of nature.

**Sect. 130.** Secondly, the power of punishing is totally given up. A person engages their natural force (which
they used to use to execute the law of nature on their own authority) to assist the executive power of the society,
as the law requires. Since they are now in a new state where they enjoy many benefits from the labor, assistance,
and society of others (as well as protection from the group's full strength), they have to give up as much of their
natural liberty in providing for themselves as the prosperity and safety of the society require. This is not only
necessary but just, since every other member of the society does the same.

**Sect. 131.** Even though people give up their equality, liberty, and executive power in the State of Nature to the
society's legislative—to be used as the good of the society requires—they do this with the intention of *better*
preserving themselves, their liberty, and their property. No rational creature would change their condition just
to make it worse. Therefore, the power of the society or legislative can never extend beyond the common good. the
government is obligated to secure everyone's property by fixing the three defects mentioned earlier that made the
State of Nature so unsafe.

Anyone who has the legislative or supreme power of a commonwealth is bound to govern by established, standing laws
that are public and known to the people—not by random, temporary decrees. They must use indifferent and upright
judges to decide controversies according to those laws, and employ the community's force at home only to execute
those laws, or abroad to prevent or fix foreign injuries and secure the community. All of this must be directed
toward one goal: the peace, safety, and public good of the people.
