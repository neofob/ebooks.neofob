# CHAPTER XIV

## OF PREROGATIVE

**Sect. 159.** In well-framed governments where the legislative and executive are separate, some things are left
to the discretion of the executive. Legislators can't foresee every possible useful action or every emergency,
so the executor has a natural right to act for the public good in cases where the municipal law hasn't given
direction, until the legislature can meet. Some things are simply impossible to legislate for. In some cases, the
laws should even yield to the executive's discretion—which is basically a return to the fundamental law of nature:
that everyone in society should be preserved as much as possible. For example, it might be necessary to pull down
an innocent man's house to stop a fire from spreading to others. A ruler should have the power to mitigate the
severity of the law and pardon offenders, because the goal of government is the preservation of all—even the
guilty should be spared if it doesn't hurt the innocent.

**Sect. 160.** This power to act based on discretion for the public good, without a specific law (and sometimes even
against the letter of the law), is called **prerogative**. It's necessary because law-making bodies are often too
few or too slow for the fast pace of execution, and because it's impossible to make laws that cover every accident
without causing harm through rigid application. Therefore, the executive is given a latitude to make choices where
the law is silent.

**Sect. 161.** As long as this power is used for the benefit of the community and consistent with the government's
goals, it's legit prerogative and nobody questions it. People aren't usually picky about it as long as it's used
for the public good. But if there's a dispute between the executive and the people about whether something is a
prerogative, the answer is simple: did the action help or hurt the people?

**Sect. 162.** In early governments, commonwealths were basically like families, and the rulers were like fathers
watching over their kids. Most of the government was just prerogative. a few established laws did the job, and
the ruler's discretion handled the rest. But when weak princes started using this power for their own private
ends instead of the public good, people started demanding express laws to limit prerogative. The people found it
necessary to define the limits of power that they had previously left to the wisdom of princes who actually used
it for the good of their people.

**Sect. 163.** People who think the public is "encroaching" on the prerogative by demanding defined laws are totally
wrong. The people aren't taking away anything that rightfully belongs to the prince; they're just declaring that
the power they gave him to act for their good isn't a right to act against it. The total goal of government is
the public good. Anything that helps that goal isn't an encroachment. If someone claims they have a right to
an arbitrary power that hurts the public, they're acting like they're not part of a society of rational beings,
but like a master of a herd of inferior creatures. Rational people don't enter societies to be harmed.

**Sect. 164.** A rational person wouldn't freely subject themselves to another for their own harm. Therefore,
prerogative is just the people's permission for their rulers to do certain things—where the law is silent or
even against the letter of the law—for the public good. A good prince who respects the trust and focuses on the
public good can't have "too much" prerogative (because doing good is always and by definition a good thing). But
a weak or bad prince who claims this power as a right to promote his own interests gives the people a reason to
limit that power, even if they were previously chill with it.

**Sect. 165.** If you look at the history of England, the wisest and best princes actually had the largest
prerogative. This is because the people saw that their actions were aimed at the public good and didn't mind when
they acted outside the letter of the law. Even when those princes made mistakes (because they're still just human),
the overall trend was the care of the public. The people were satisfied and let them expand their prerogative
because they knew it was being used to help them.

**Sect. 166.** Some might say these god-like princes actually had a title to arbitrary power, essentially making
absolute monarchy a good thing because it mirrors how God governs the universe. This is exactly why the reigns of
good princes are the most dangerous for liberty: their successors might assume that what was done *for the good of
the people* was a *right* of the ruler to do regardless of the outcome. This leads to disputes and disorder when
the people have to fight to prove that the ruler doesn'T have a right to cause harm. No one in a society ever has
a right to harm others, but it's reasonable for people to not set limits on a ruler who never exceeds the bounds
of the public good. Prerogative is simply the power to do public good without a rule.

**Sect. 167.** The power to call parliament (timing, place, and duration) is a prerogative of the king, but it
comes with the trust that it'll be used for the nation's good. Since it's impossible to predict the perfect time
or place for every assembly, this choice is left to the executive’s prudence.

**Sect. 168.** The big question: Who judges if prerogative is being used correctly? Between an executive and a
legislative that depends on the executive to be called, there's no judge on earth. The same is true between the
legislative and the people if the rulers try to enslave them. In these cases, the people have only one remedy:
an appeal to heaven. This is the ultimate determination. Rulers who exercise power for the harm of the people
are acting without authority, since nobody consents to be ruled for their own harm. Whenever a person or group is
deprived of their rights and has no appeal on earth, they have the liberty to appeal to heaven when the cause is
serious enough. This doesn't mean constant disorder; it only happens when the majority feels that the situation
is unbearable and must be fixed. Wise princes avoid this at all costs—it's the most dangerous thing that can
happen to them.
