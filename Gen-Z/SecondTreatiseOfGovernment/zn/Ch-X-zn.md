# CHAPTER X

## OF THE FORMS OF A COMMONWEALTH

**Sect. 132.** As we've seen, when people first unite into a society, the majority naturally holds the power of the
community. They can use that power to make laws for the community over time and execute them through officers they
appoint; in that case, the government is a perfect democracy. Or, they can put the law-making power in the hands
of a few select people and their successors; then it's an oligarchy. Or, they can put it in the hands of one man,
which is a monarchy. If it's him and his heirs, it's a hereditary monarchy. If it's only for his life, and then
he just nominates a successor to give the power back to the people, it's an elective monarchy. The community can
also create mixed forms of government. If the legislative power is first given to one or more people for their
lives or a limited time, and then the supreme power reverts to the community, the community can then decide how
to distribute that power again and start a new form of government. Since the form of government depends on where
the supreme power (the legislative) is placed—and since an inferior power can't tell a superior what to do—the
placement of the law-making power defines the form of the commonwealth.

**Sect. 133.** Throughout this, when I say "commonwealth," I don't mean a democracy or any specific form of
government. I mean any independent community (what the Latins called *civitas*). The word "commonwealth" is the best
English equivalent for this kind of society; the words "community" or "city" don't quite fit. There can be smaller
communities within a government, and in our language, a "city" is a different concept from a "commonwealth." To
avoid any confusion, I'll use the word "commonwealth" the way King James I used it, which I believe is its genuine
meaning. If anyone dislikes it, I'm open to a better word.
