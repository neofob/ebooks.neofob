# CHAPTER XIX

## OF THE DISSOLUTION OF GOVERNMENT

**Sect. 211.** Anyone who wants to talk clearly about the dissolution of government must first distinguish between
the dissolution of the *society* and the dissolution of the *government*.

What creates a community and brings people out of the loose State of Nature (the OG state) into one political society
is the agreement where everyone agrees to incorporate and act as one body—becoming one distinct commonwealth. The
usual, and almost only, way this union is dissolved is through the inroad of foreign force—a conquest. In that case,
because they can't maintain themselves as one independent body, the union of that body necessarily ceases. Everyone
returns to the state they were in before, free to shift for themselves and provide for their own safety in some
other society.

Whenever a society is dissolved, it is certain that the government of that society cannot remain. Conquerors'
swords often rip governments up by the roots and mangle societies to pieces, separating the subdued multitude
from the protection of the society that should have preserved them from violence. The world knows this way of
dissolving governments all too well; it doesn't take much to prove that where the society is gone, the government
cannot stay. It is as impossible as the frame of a house subsisting when its materials have been scattered by a
whirlwind or jumbled into a heap by an earthquake.

**Sect. 212.** Besides being overturned from without, governments are dissolved from within.

First, when the legislative is altered. Civil society is a state of peace among its members, where the state of
war is excluded by the "umpirage" (arbitration) provided in their legislative to end all differences. It is in
the legislative that the members of a commonwealth are united into one coherent, living body. This is the soul
that gives form, life, and unity to the commonwealth; it's where members find their mutual influence, sympathy,
and connection. Therefore, when the legislative is broken or dissolved, dissolution and death follow. Because the
essence of society consists in having one will, the legislative—once established by the majority—is the voice
and keeper of that will.

The constitution of the legislative is the first and fundamental act of society. It provides for the continuation
of their union under the direction of persons and the bonds of laws, created by people authorized by the consent
and appointment of the people. Without this, no one man or group among them has the authority to make laws that
are binding to the rest. When anyone takes it upon themselves to make laws without being appointed by the people,
they are making laws without authority, which the people are not bound to obey. Consequently, the people return
to a state of non-subjection and may constitute a new legislative for themselves as they see fit, being entirely
free to resist the force of those who would impose their will without authority. Everyone is at the disposal of
their own will when those who were delegated to declare the public will are excluded, and others who have no such
authority usurp their place.

**Sect. 213.** Since this is usually brought about by those in the commonwealth who misuse their power, it's hard
to analyze correctly without knowing the form of government. Let's suppose the legislative power is shared among
three distinct parts:

1. A single hereditary person with constant, supreme executive power, and the power to call or dissolve the other
two at certain times.
2. An assembly of hereditary nobility.
3. An assembly of representatives chosen for a set time by the people.

With this setup, it's evident that:

**Sect. 214.** First, when such a prince sets up his own arbitrary will in place of the laws (which are the will
of the society declared by the legislative), then the legislative is changed. Because the rules and laws being
executed and enforced are no longer those enacted by the legislative constituted by the society, the legislative
has been effectively replaced. Whoever introduces new laws without authorization from the society's fundamental
appointment, or subverts the old ones, disowns and overturns the power by which they were made, thereby establishing
a new (unlawful) legislative.

**Sect. 215.** Secondly, when the prince prevents the legislative from assembling on time or from acting freely toward
the goals for which it was created, the legislative is altered. A legislative isn't just a certain number of men or
their meeting; it is the freedom to debate and the time to perfect what is for the good of the society. When these
are taken away, depriving society of the exercise of its power, the legislative is truly altered. Governments are
not constituted by names, but by the actual use and exercise of the powers intended to accompany those names. He
who removes the freedom or hinders the acting of the legislative effectively destroys the legislative and puts an
end to the government.

**Sect. 216.** Thirdly, when the prince uses arbitrary power to alter the electors or the ways of election without
the consent and against the common interest of the people, the legislative is also altered. If people other than
those authorized by society choose the leaders, or do so in a way other than what society prescribed, then those
chosen are not the legislative appointed by the people.

**Sect. 217.** Fourthly, delivering the people into the subjection of a foreign power—whether by the prince or
the legislative—is certainly a change of the legislative and therefore a dissolution of the government. The reason
people entered into society was to be preserved as one entire, free, independent society governed by its own laws;
this goal is lost the moment they are handed over to the power of another.

**Sect. 218.** In this constitution, the dissolution of government in these cases is the fault of the prince. Because
he controls the force, the treasure, and the offices of the state—and is often persuaded (or flattered) that as
supreme magistrate he is above control—he alone is in a position to push for these changes under the guise of
lawful authority. He can use his power to terrify or suppress opposers as "factious" or "enemies of the government."

No other part of the legislative or the people could attempt such an alteration without open and visible rebellion,
which would be noticed and likely fail. Furthermore, because the prince has the power to dissolve the other parts
of the legislative, they can never alter the legislative without his concurrence. However, if other parts of
the legislative contribute to such attempts—either by promoting them or failing to hinder them—they are also
guilty. This is undoubtedly the greatest crime one human can commit against another.

**Sec. 219.** There is one more way such a government may be dissolved: when the supreme executive power neglects
and abandons his charge so that the laws already made can no longer be executed. This is a direct path to anarchy
and effectively dissolves the government. Laws are not made for their own sake, but to be the bonds of society,
keeping every part of the body politic in its proper place and function. When execution totally ceases, the
government visibly ceases, and the people become a confused multitude without order or connection. Where there
is no longer the administration of justice to secure rights, nor any power to direct force or provide for public
necessities, there is simply no government left. A government without laws is a political mystery, unconceivable
to humans and inconsistent with human society.

**Sect. 220.** In these cases, when the government is dissolved, the people are free to provide for themselves by
erecting a new legislative—differing in persons, form, or both—as they find most necessary for their safety
and good. Society can never, through the fault of another, lose its native and original right to preserve itself,
which is only possible through a settled legislative and the fair, impartial execution of its laws.

Mankind is not so miserable that they cannot use this remedy before it's too late. Telling people they can provide
for themselves *after* their old government is gone due to oppression or foreign takeover is like telling them they
can expect relief only when the evil is incurable. It's essentially telling them to be slaves first and then take
care of their liberty once the chains are already on. That's not relief; it's mockery. People cannot be secure from
tyranny if there's no way to escape it until they are perfectly crushed; therefore, they have not only a right to
get out of it but a right to *prevent* it.

**Sect. 221.** Therefore, there is a second way governments are dissolved: when the legislative, or the prince
(or both), act contrary to their trust.

First, the legislative acts against its trust when it tries to invade the property of the subject, or attempts to make
itself (or any part of the community) the master or arbitrary disposer of the people's lives, liberties, or fortunes.

**Sect. 222.** The reason people enter into society is the preservation of their property. The goal in authorizing
a legislative is that laws and rules be made as guards and fences for the property of all members, limiting the
power of every part of society. It can never be the will of society that the legislative has the power to destroy
that which everyone sought to secure by entering society in the first place. Whenever legislators try to take away
and destroy the property of the people, or reduce them to slavery under arbitrary power, they put themselves into
a state of war with the people. The people are then absolved from any further obedience and are left to the common
refuge God has provided for all against force and violence.

Whenever the legislative transgresses this fundamental rule—whether through ambition, fear, folly, or
corruption—and tries to grab absolute power over the lives, liberties, and estates of the people, they forfeit
the power the people gave them. That power devolves back to the people, who have a right to resume their original
liberty and establish a new legislative to provide for their own safety and security.

This also applies to the supreme executor. Having a double trust—to be part of the legislative and the supreme
executor of the law—he acts against both when he tries to set up his own arbitrary will as the law. He also
acts against his trust when he uses the force, treasure, and offices of society to corrupt representatives or
pre-select electors through threats and promises, ensuring a legislative that will vote however he wants. To rig
candidates and elections in this way is to rip the government up by the roots and poison the fountain of public
security. The people reserved the choice of representatives as a fence for their properties so that they could
be freely chosen to act for the public good after mature debate. Anyone who prepares an assembly of "yes-men"
is committing a massive breach of trust and declaring a design to subvert the government. If you add rewards,
punishments, and the use of perverted laws to destroy anyone who resists, the intention is clear. Anyone who
employs power contrary to its original trust cannot be trusted.

**Sect. 223.** Some might argue that because the people are ignorant and discontented, basing government on their
"unsteady opinion" is a recipe for ruin, and that no government can last if the people can just replace the legislative
whenever they're offended. I answer: the opposite is true. People don't abandon their old forms as easily as some
suggest. They are hard to convince to change even acknowledged faults in the frame they're used to. Even when the
whole world sees an opportunity for change, people are slow to move. This aversion to quitting old constitutions
has kept this kingdom—through many revolutions—tied to its old legislative of King, Lords, and Commons. Even
when the crown was taken from some princes, the people never went so far as to change the line of succession.

**Sect. 224.** Others will say this hypothesis encourages frequent rebellion. My answer:

First, it encourages no more rebellion than any other hypothesis. When people are made miserable and exposed to
arbitrary power, it doesn't matter if you call their governors "sons of Jupiter" or claim they are divine—it's
the same result. People who are generally ill-treated and denied their rights will look for any opportunity to
ease the burden. This is a universal truth seen in all sorts of governments worldwide.

**Sect. 225.** Secondly, revolutions don't happen over every little mismanagement. Great mistakes, inconvenient
laws, and human frailty are generally borne by the people without mutiny. But when a *long train* of abuses, lies,
and tricks all point in the same direction, making the design visible, people will eventually wake up and try to
put the rule into hands that can actually secure the goals of government. Without this, old names and fancy forms
are actually worse than the state of nature or pure anarchy, because the inconveniences are the same but the remedy
is much farther off.

**Sect. 226.** Thirdly, this doctrine—that people can provide for their own safety via a new legislative when
their leaders betray their trust—is actually the *best* fence against rebellion. Rebellion is not an opposition
to people, but to *authority* founded in laws. Those who use force to break and violate those laws are the true
rebels. When people enter society, they swap force for laws to preserve property and peace. Those who bring back
force in opposition to the law are "rebellare"—bringing back the state of war. Those in power are the most
likely to do this, tempted by their authority and force. The best way to prevent this is to show them the danger
and injustice of it.

**Sect. 227.** In both cases—whether the legislative is changed or the legislators act against their trust—those
who cause it are guilty of rebellion. If someone uses force to take away the established legislative, they are
removing the "umpirage" (arbitration) everyone agreed to for peace, thereby re-introducing the state of war. By
destroying the only authority the people authorized, they introduce a power of raw force. Likewise, legislators who
were set up to protect the people but instead invade their liberties and properties are putting themselves into a
state of war with those they were supposed to protect. They are, with the greatest aggravation, "rebellantes"—rebels.

**Sect. 228.** If those who claim this leads to rebellion mean that it might cause civil wars, they are essentially
saying that honest men should not oppose robbers or pirates because it might cause disorder. If the innocent
must quietly hand over everything they own to a violent attacker for the sake of "peace," then that is a peace
consisting only of violence and robbery—a peace that only benefits the oppressors. It would be like a lamb
yielding its throat to a wolf. Polyphemus's den is a perfect example of such a "peace," where Ulysses and his men
were expected to just let themselves be eaten. I'm sure a "prudent" man like Ulysses would have preached passive
obedience in that moment to avoid the "inconvenience" of resisting.

**Sect. 229.** The goal of government is the good of mankind. What is better for mankind: to be always exposed to
the limitless will of tyranny, or for rulers to occasionally be opposed when they use their power for destruction
instead of preservation?

**Sect. 230.** And let no one say that this will lead to chaos because of some "busy head" or turbulent spirit who
wants to change the government. Such people might stir up trouble, but it will only lead to their own ruin. Until the
mischief becomes general and the rulers' evil designs become visible to the majority, people—who are generally
more disposed to suffer than to resist—won't move. Individual cases of injustice don't move the masses. But
if there is a universal conviction grounded in manifest evidence that their liberties are under attack, how can
they be stopped from resisting? If they are blamed for having the sense to see things as they feel them, then the
blame truly lies with the rulers who put things in such a position. I'll admit that the ambition of private men
has caused disorder, but whether disorder usually starts from the people's desire to cast off lawful authority, or
from the rulers' desire to exercise arbitrary power, I'll leave to impartial history. This I am sure: whoever uses
force to invade the rights of the prince or the people, and lays the foundation for overturning a just government,
is guilty of the greatest possible crime. They are responsible for the blood and desolation that follow and should
be treated as the common enemy and pest of mankind.

**Sect. 231.** Everyone agrees that foreigners or subjects attempting to seize property by force can be resisted. Yet
recently, some have denied that magistrates doing the same can be resisted—as if those with the greatest legal
privileges have a power to break the very laws that gave them those privileges. Their offense is actually greater
because they are ungrateful for their share and betray the trust placed in them by their brethren.

**Sect. 232.** Whoever uses force without right puts himself into a state of war with those against whom he
uses it. In that state, all previous ties are cancelled, and everyone has a right to defend themselves. Even
Barclay—the great defender of the sacredness of kings—admitted that it is lawful for people, in some cases,
to resist their king. He basically argues that since they can resist in some cases, not all resistance is rebellion.

**Sect. 233.** To answer a possible question: Must the people always just accept the cruelty of tyranny? Must they
watch their cities burn and their families be destroyed while they sit still? Must humans be denied the natural
privilege of opposing force with force? I answer: Self-defense is part of the law of nature. It cannot be denied
to the community, even against the king. However, seeking *revenge* is not allowed. If a king treats the whole
commonwealth with intolerable cruelty, the people have a right to resist and defend themselves. But they must only
*defend*—they must not attack the prince or seek revenge for past violences, as punishing a superior is against
nature. They can prevent future harm, but they cannot punish the king for past crimes. This is the privilege of
the collective people over the individual: while individuals might have to be patient, the body of the people can
resist intolerable tyranny.

**Sect. 234.** This is as far as even the great advocates of absolute monarchy allow.

**Sect. 235.** However, Barclay adds two limitations: it must be done with "reverence," and without "punishment." But
how do you resist force without striking back, or how do you strike with "reverence"? Trying to oppose an attack
with only a shield, in a "respectful" posture, without a sword to stop the assailant, is just a fast way to get
killed. It's as ridiculous as Juvenal's description of fighting: "You hit, I just get hit." Anyone who resists must
be allowed to strike. If the author wants to combine blows with "reverence," he can ask for a "respectful cudgeling."

Secondly, the claim that an inferior cannot punish a superior is generally true—while he is a superior. But
when force is met with force, it's a state of war that levels the field. All former relations of reverence and
superiority are cancelled. Now, the one who opposes the unjust aggressor has the superiority: the right to punish
the offender for the breach of peace and all resulting evils. Barclay eventually contradicts himself elsewhere,
saying a king can "un-king" himself in two cases:

1. If he tries to destroy the kingdom (like Nero, who wanted to burn Rome and leave, or Caligula). When a king
seriously plots to ruin the commonwealth, he forfeits the power to govern.
2. If he makes himself a dependent of another and hands his kingdom over to a foreign power. By alienating the
kingdom, he loses his power and leaves the people free.

**Sect. 237.** In short, there are cases where the people can and should take arms against a domineering king. While
you should honor the king, if he does something that makes him cease to be a king—meaning he divests himself of
the crown and returns to being a private citizen—the people become superior and regain the power they had before
they crowned him.

**Sect. 239.** Even the champions of absolute monarchy admit a king can be resisted. Basically, wherever there is no
authority, there is no king. The core principle is the *breach of trust*: failing to preserve the government and the
public good. When a king puts himself in a state of war with his people, they can prosecute him like any other man who
started a war. Even Barclay admits that the people can prevent a designed tyranny before it happens. The failure to
maintain the public good is evidence of such a design. The fault is the loss of liberty, regardless of whether the
new master is a fellow citizen or a foreigner. There are many examples in history showing that it's the change in
*government* (to tyranny), not the change in *nationality* of the ruler, that is the offense. Writers like Hooker,
Fortescue, and Bracton all align with the idea that princes can forfeit their power. Those who try to use Hooker's
work to justify absolute tyranny are just tools of cunning men, pulling down their own fabric. History will show
that this "new" civil policy is dangerous and destructive. Future generations will abhor the memory of those servile
flatterers who tried to turn all government into absolute tyranny and treat all men as if they were born for slavery.

**Sect. 240.** The common question is: Who shall be the judge of whether the prince or legislative has betrayed their
trust? Some might say this is just a tool for "factious men" to use when the prince is just using his legitimate
prerogative. My answer: The people shall be the judge. Who better to judge a trustee's performance than the one
who delegated the trust in the first place? If this is true for private citizens, it is certainly true for the
welfare of millions.

**Sect. 241.** Furthermore, this doesn't mean there is no judge. Where there is no earthly judge, God in heaven
is the judge. He is the judge of the *right*. But every individual is the judge for themselves whether another
has put them into a state of war and whether they should appeal to the Supreme Judge.

**Sect. 242.** If a controversy arises between a prince and the people over a matter where the law is silent and
the consequences are huge, the proper umpire should be the body of the people. Since they lodged the trust in the
prince, they are the only ones who can determine how far that trust was meant to extend. If the prince refuses
this determination, the only appeal left is to heaven. Force between parties with no known superior on earth is
a state of war, and the injured party must decide for themselves when to make that appeal.

**Sect. 243.** To conclude: The power each individual gave to the society upon entering it never reverts
to the individuals as long as the society lasts; it remains in the community. Otherwise, there would be no
commonwealth. Likewise, once the people have established a legislative with the power to continue and provide
successors, the political power stays with the legislative as long as the government lasts.

However, if the legislative was only temporary, or if it is forfeited through the miscarriages of those in authority,
the power reverts to the society. The people then have the right to act as supreme, continuing the legislative as
they see fit or erecting a new form of government entirely to ensure their own safety and security.
