# CHAPTER XII

## THE LEGISLATIVE, EXECUTIVE, AND FEDERATIVE POWERS OF THE COMMONWEALTH

**Sect. 143.** The legislative power is the right to direct how the commonwealth's force is used to preserve
the community and its members. Since the laws are meant to be constant and long-term, the legislature doesn't
need to be in session all the time. Also, giving the same people the power to both *make* the laws and *execute*
them is way too big a temptation; they might exempt themselves from their own laws and twist things for their
own private advantage, which goes against the whole point of society. That's why in well-ordered commonwealths,
the legislative power is given to a group of people who meet, make the laws, and then separate, becoming subjects
to the very laws they just created. This forces them to make laws for the public good.

**Sect. 144.** But since laws need constant enforcement, there has to be a power that's always active to make sure
they're followed. This is why the legislative and executive powers are often separate.

**Sect. 145.** There's another power in every commonwealth that's basically a "natural" power, because it's
what every individual had before joining society. While members of a commonwealth are distinct persons bound by
society's laws *internally*, they are one single body compared to the rest of the world (which is still in the
State of Nature). Therefore, any conflict between a member of the society and an outsider is handled by the public;
an injury to one member engages the whole group for reparation. In this sense, the entire community is one body
in the State of Nature relative to everyone outside of it.

**Sect. 146.** This includes the power of war and peace, treaties, alliances, and all dealings with outside people
or communities. This can be called the "federative" power.

**Sect. 147.** The executive and federative powers are distinct, but they're almost always united. The executive
handles internal municipal laws; the federative handles external security and interests. The federative power is
harder to direct with standing laws because it depends on the unpredictable actions of foreigners. Therefore, it
has to be left to the prudence and wisdom of those in charge to manage it for the public good, using their best skills.

**Sect. 148.** Even though the executive and federative powers are theoretically distinct, they're almost impossible
to separate in practice because they both require the society's physical force. You can't really put the force of
the commonwealth in two different, uncoordinated hands; that would just lead to chaos and ruin.
