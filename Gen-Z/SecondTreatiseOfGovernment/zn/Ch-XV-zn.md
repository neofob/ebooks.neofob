# CHAPTER XV

## PATERNAL, POLITICAL, AND DESPOTICAL POWER, CONSIDERED TOGETHER

**Sect. 169.** I've talked about these separately, but since people keep confusing them and making mistakes about
government, it's probably a good idea to look at them together.

**Sect. 170.** First, **Paternal (or parental) power** is what parents have over their children to govern them
for their own good until they reach the age of reason or have enough knowledge to follow the law of nature or the
laws of their country. The love and tenderness parents feel for their kids shows that this isn't meant to be a
severe, arbitrary government, but a way to help, instruct, and protect their offspring. This power doesn't give a
parent the right to kill their children and doesn't keep a child in subjection once they're an adult—beyond the
obligation of respect, honor, and support for the parents. Paternal power is natural, but it doesn't reach into
the jurisdiction of political power. A father has no right to the property of his adult child.

**Sect. 171.** Second, **Political power** is the power that every person in the State of Nature (the OG state)
gives up to society and its governors, under the trust that it will be used for their good and the preservation
of their property. In the State of Nature, you have the power to do what you think is best for your survival and
to punish others' crimes to preserve yourself and mankind. When you join a society, you hand that power over. The
goal of the magistrate is to preserve the lives, liberties, and possessions of the members. It cannot be absolute
or arbitrary over those things; instead, it's a power to make laws and attach penalties that preserve the whole
by removing the "corrupt parts" (criminals) who threaten the healthy ones. This power only comes from a compact
and the mutual consent of the community.

**Sect. 172.** Third, **Despotical power** is an absolute, arbitrary power one person has over another, including the
power to take their life whenever they want. This isn't a power given by nature (since no one is naturally superior)
or by contract (since you can't contract away your own life). It only happens through forfeiture: when an aggressor
puts themselves into a state of war, they renounce reason and the common bond of humanity. By choosing force over
peace to achieve unjust ends, they behave like a wild beast and become liable to be destroyed by the injured party
or the rest of mankind. Only captives taken in a just war are subject to despotical power. This is a continuation
of the state of war. No compact can be made with someone who isn't master of their own life. As soon as a compact
is made, slavery ends and the absolute power ceases.

**Sect. 173.** To summarize:
- Nature gives **paternal power** to parents for the benefit of children during minority, to help them manage
their property.
- Voluntary agreement gives **political power** to governors for the benefit of subjects, to secure their property.
- Forfeiture gives **despotical power** to lords for their own benefit over those who have lost all property
(their lives and liberties).

**Sect. 174.** If you look at how these powers start and what they're for, it's clear that paternal power is
far below political power, and despotical power is way beyond it. Absolute dominion (despotism) is completely
inconsistent with civil society—it's as opposite as slavery is to property. Paternal power exists where a child
can't manage their property; political power exists where people can dispose of their own property; and despotical
power exists over those who have no property at all.
