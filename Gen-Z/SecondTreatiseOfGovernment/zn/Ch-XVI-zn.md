# CHAPTER XVI

## OF CONQUEST

**Sect. 175.** While it's been established that governments can only truly begin as mentioned before, and that
political structures are built on nothing but the consent of the people; nevertheless, the chaos unleashed
by ambition has so saturated the world that in the deafening noise of war—which defines so much of human
history—this consent is barely acknowledged. Consequently, many have mistaken the raw force of arms for the
actual consent of the people, treating conquest as one of the foundations of government.

But conquest is as far from establishing a legitimate government as demolishing a house is from building a new one
in its place. Indeed, it often clears the way for a new commonwealth by erasing the old; but without the consent
of the people, it can never actually erect a new one.

**Sect. 176.** It is an easily accepted truth that an aggressor, who enters a state of war with another and unjustly
invades another's rights, can never, through such an unjust war, acquire a legitimate right over the conquered. No
one would argue that robbers and pirates have a right of empire over whoever they have the force to master, or
that people are bound by promises extorted from them through unlawful force.

If a robber breaks into my house and, with a dagger at my throat, forces me to sign deeds conveying my estate to
him, would this give him any legal title? Exactly such a title, by his sword, is what an unjust conqueror claims
when he forces me into submission. The injury and the crime are identical, whether committed by someone wearing
a crown or some petty villain. The title of the offender, or the size of his army, does not change the nature of
the offense—if anything, it only aggravates it. The only difference is that "great robbers" punish "little ones"
to keep them obedient; while the great ones are rewarded with laurels and triumphs because they are simply too
powerful for the weak hands of earthly justice.

What is my remedy against a robber who breaks into my home? I appeal to the law for justice. But perhaps justice
is denied, or I am crippled and unable to move, robbed of the means to pursue it. If God has removed all earthly
remedies, only patience remains. But my son, when able, may seek the relief of the law that I was denied; he or
his son may renew the appeal until the right is recovered.

However, the conquered, and their children, often have no court and no arbitrator on earth to appeal to. In that case,
they may appeal—as Jephtha did—to heaven, repeating their appeal until they recover the native right of their
ancestors: the right to have a legislative power over them that the majority approves of and freely acquiesces in.

If the objection is raised that "this would cause endless trouble," I answer that justice itself causes no more
trouble when she is open to all who appeal to her. He who troubles his neighbor without cause is punished by
the court he appeals to; and he who appeals to heaven must be certain he has the right on his side—and a right
substantial enough to justify the cost of the appeal—since he will answer at a tribunal that cannot be deceived
and which will surely retribute to every one according to the mischiefs they created for their fellow humans. Thus,
it is clear that he who conquers in an unjust war can have no legitimate title to the subjection and obedience of
the conquered.

**Sect. 177.** But supposing victory favors the right side, let us consider a conqueror in a lawful war, and see
what power he actually acquires, and over whom.

First, it is plain he gains no power via conquest over those who fought on his side. Those who fought with him cannot
suffer from the conquest; they must remain at least as many freemen as they were before. Most commonly, they serve
on terms to share in the spoils and other advantages of the conquering sword, or at least have a portion of the
subdued country bestowed upon them. The conquering people are not, I hope, to become slaves by conquest, wearing
their laurels only to show they are sacrifices to their leader's triumph. Those who base absolute monarchy on the
"title of the sword" turn their founding heroes into mere mercenaries and forget they had officers and soldiers
who fought beside them, assisted in the subduing, or shared in possessing the lands they mastered.

Some claim the English monarchy is founded on the Norman conquest, and that our princes thereby have a title to
absolute dominion. Even if this were true (which history suggests otherwise), and even if William had a right to
make war on this island, his dominion by conquest could reach no further than the Saxons and Britons who lived
here then. The Normans who came with him, and all descended from them, are freemen, not subjects by conquest. If
I, or anyone else, claim freedom derived from them, it would be very hard to prove the contrary; and clearly, the
law—which makes no distinction between the two—intends that there be no difference in their freedom or privileges.

**Sect. 188.** But supposing—which is rare—that the conquerors and conquered never merge into one people
under the same laws and freedom; let us see what power a lawful conqueror has over the subdued. I say this power
is purely despotical. He has absolute power over the lives of those who, by an unjust war, have forfeited them;
but not over the lives or fortunes of those who did not engage in the war, nor over the possessions even of those
who were actually engaged in it.

**Sect. 179.** Secondly, I maintain that the conqueror gains no power except over those who actually assisted,
concurred, or consented to the unjust force used against him. Since the people gave their governors no power to do
an unjust thing, such as waging an unjust war (for the people never had such power themselves), they should not be
held guilty of the violence and injustice committed in an unjust war, any further than they actually aided it. They
are no more guilty of the violence their governors use upon the people than they are of any other oppression,
having empowered them to neither.

Conquerors, it is true, seldom bother to make this distinction, allowing the confusion of war to sweep everyone
together; but this does not alter the right. Since the conqueror's power over the lives of the conquered exists
only because they used force to maintain an injustice, he can have that power only over those who concurred in
that force. All others are innocent. He has no more title over the people of that country, who did him no injury
and thus made no forfeiture of their lives, than he has over any other person who lived on fair terms with him
without provocation.

**Sect. 180.** Thirdly, the power a conqueror gains over those he overcomes in a just war is perfectly despotical:
he has absolute power over the lives of those who, by putting themselves in a state of war, have forfeited them;
but he has no right or title to their possessions.

I realize this may seem like a strange doctrine at first sight, as it is entirely contrary to the practice of the
world. Nothing is more common in discussions of the dominion of countries than to say one "conquered" it, as if
conquest automatically conveyed a right of possession. But we must consider that the practice of the strong and
powerful, however universal it may be, is seldom the rule of right—even if it is the case that the conquered
cannot easily argue against the conditions cut out for them by the conquering sword.

**Sect. 181.** Although in all war there is usually a mix of force and damage, and the aggressor seldom fails
to harm the estate when using force against people, it is the *use of force only* that puts a man into the state
of war. Whether by force he begins the injury, or—having quietly used fraud to do the injury—refuses to make
reparation and uses force to maintain it (which is the same as having done it by force from the start), it is the
unjust use of force that creates the war.

Whether a man breaks open my house and violently throws me out, or peaceably enters and then by force keeps me
out, he does the same thing—assuming we are in a state where there is no common judge on earth to whom I may
appeal and to whom we are both obliged to submit. It is the unjust use of force that puts a man into the state of
war with another; and thereby, the guilty party forfeits his life. By abandoning reason—the rule given between
humans—and using force, the way of beasts, he becomes liable to be destroyed by the one he uses force against,
like any savage ravenous beast dangerous to existence.

**Sect. 182.** But because the failures of the father are not the faults of the children—and children may be
rational and peaceable despite the brutishness of the father—the father, through his violence, can forfeit only
his own life; he does not involve his children in his guilt or destruction.

His goods, which nature (seeking the preservation of all mankind) has made belong to the children to keep them from
perishing, continue to belong to them. Supposing the children did not join in the war—due to infancy, absence,
or choice—they did any nothing to forfeit them. Nor does the conqueror have any right to take them away simply
by the title of having subdued the father. Though he may have some right to them to repair damages sustained during
the war, we shall see the extent of that shortly.

Thus, he who by conquest has a right over a man's person to destroy him if he pleases, does NOT thereby have a
right over his estate to possess and enjoy it. It is the brutal force the aggressor used that gives his adversary
a right to take his life, treating him as a noxious creature; but only *damage sustained* gives title to another
man's goods. For while I may kill a thief who attacks me on the highway, I may not (which seems less severe)
take his money and let him go; that would be robbery on my part. His force and the state of war he entered made
him forfeit his life, but gave me no title to his goods.

The right of conquest extends only to the lives of those who joined in the war, not to their estates, except only
to make reparation for damages received and the costs of war—and even then, with reservation of the rights of
the innocent wife and children.

**Sect. 183.** Even if the conqueror has maximal justice on his side, he has no right to seize more than the
vanquished could forfeit. The vanquished's life is at the victor's mercy; his service and goods may be appropriated
for reparation; but he cannot take the goods of the wife and children. They too had a title to the goods he enjoyed.

For example, if I in the state of nature (and all commonwealths are in the state of nature toward one another)
injure another man and refuse satisfaction, it leads to a state of war where my use of force to defend what
I unjustly took makes me the aggressor. I am conquered: my life is forfeit and at mercy, but not my wife's or
children's. They did not make the war, nor assist in it. I could not forfeit their lives; they were not mine to
forfeit. My wife had a share in my estate that I could not forfeit. My children, born of me, had a right to be
maintained by my labor or substance.

Here is the case: the conqueror has a title to reparation for damages, and the children have a title to their
father's estate for their subsistence. Regarding the wife's share—whether her own labor or a compact gave her
title—her husband could not forfeit what was hers.

What must be done? I answer: since the fundamental law of nature is that all, as much as possible, should be
preserved, it follows that if there is not enough to satisfy both—the conqueror's losses and the children's
maintenance—the one who has plenty must remit some of his full satisfaction to give way to the pressing and
preferable title of those who are in danger of perishing without it.

**Sect. 184.** Even if the costs and damages of war are paid to the conqueror to the last penny, and the children
of the vanquished, spoiled of all goods, are left to starve; the satisfaction of the conqueror's due will hardly
give him a title to any country he conquers. The damages of war can rarely amount to the value of any considerable
tract of land in a world where all land is possessed.

If I have not taken the conqueror's land (which, being vanquished, is impossible), hardly any other spoil I caused
could equal the value of my own land, assuming equal cultivation and scale. The destruction of a year's product or
two is the most that is usually done. As for money, riches, and treasure—these are not nature's goods; they have
a "fantastical imaginary value." Nature assigns them no such value; they are no more significant by her standard
than wampum would be to a European prince, or European silver would have been to an ancient American.

Five years of product is not worth the perpetual inheritance of land. This is easily granted if one removes the
imaginary value of money; the disproportion is greater than five to five hundred. Of course, half a year's product is
worth more than an inheritance where there is more land than inhabitants possess (where anyone can use the waste);
but in such cases, conquerors rarely care to possess the lands of the vanquished.

Therefore, no damage that people in the state of nature (as all princes and governments are relative to one another)
suffer from each other can give a conqueror power to dispossess the posterity of the vanquished and turn them
out of an inheritance that should belong to them and their descendants for all generations. The conqueror will
certainly think himself master; and it is the condition of the subdued that they cannot dispute their right. But
if that is all, it gives no more title than bare force gives the stronger over the weaker: and by that logic,
the strongest would have a right to seize whatever he pleases.

**Sect. 185.** Over those who joined with him in the war, over those of the subdued country who did not oppose
him, and even over the posterity of those who did, the conqueror—even in a just war—has no right of dominion
by conquest. They are free from any subjection to him; and if their former government is dissolved, they are at
liberty to begin and erect another for themselves.

**Sect. 186.** The conqueror, it is true, usually uses the force he has over them to compel them—with a sword
at their breasts—to accept his conditions and submit to whatever government he chooses. But the question is,
what *right* does he have to do so?

If it is said they submit by their own consent, then this admits that their consent is necessary to give the
conqueror a title to rule. It remains to be considered whether promises extorted by force, without right, can be
considered consent, and how far they bind. I say they do not bind at all; because whatever another gets from me
by force, I still retain the right to, and he is obliged to restore it immediately. He who forces my horse from
me must restore him; I still have a right to retake him.

By the same reason, he who forces a promise from me must restore it—that is, release me from the obligation;
or I may resume the choice of whether to perform it. For the law of nature, which obliges me only by the rules she
prescribes, cannot oblige me by the violation of her rules; and extorting something by force is such a violation. It
does not change the case to say I gave my promise, any more than it excuses the force when I put my hand in my
pocket and deliver my purse to a thief who demands it with a pistol at my breast.

**Sect. 187.** From all this it follows that the government of a conqueror, imposed by force on the subdued—against
whom he had no right of war, or who did not join in the war where he had a right—has no obligation upon them.

**Sect. 188.** But let us suppose that all the men of that community, being members of the same body politic,
may be taken to have joined in that unjust war, and so their lives are at the mercy of the conqueror.

**Sect. 189.** I say this does not concern their children who are minors. Since a father does not have power over the
life or liberty of his child, no act of his can possibly forfeit it. Thus, the children, regardless of what happened
to the fathers, are freemen. The absolute power of the conqueror reaches only to the persons of the men who were
subdued, and dies with them. Even if he governs the men as slaves under absolute arbitrary power, he has no such
right of dominion over their children. He can have no power over them except by their own consent, regardless of
what he forces them to say or do; and he has no lawful authority while force, and not choice, compels their submission.

**Sect. 190.** Every man is born with a double right: first, a right of freedom to his person, which no other
man has power over, and the free disposal of which lies in himself. Secondly, a right, before any other man,
to inherit with his brethren his father’s goods.

**Sect. 191.** By the first of these, a man is naturally free from subjection to any government, even if he is born
in a place under its jurisdiction. But if he disclaims the lawful government of the country he was born in, he must
also quit the right that belonged to him by its laws, and the possessions descending to him from his ancestors,
provided it was a government made by their consent.

**Sect. 192.** By the second, the inhabitants of any country who are descended from and derive title to their
estates from those who were subdued and had a government forced upon them without free consent, retain a right
to the possession of their ancestors. This holds even if they do not freely consent to the government whose hard
conditions were forced upon them. Since the first conqueror never had a title to the land, the descendants of those
forced to submit to the yoke of government by constraint always have a right to shake it off and free themselves
from the usurpation or tyranny brought by the sword, until their rulers put them under a frame of government they
willingly and by choice consent to.

Who doubts that the Grecian Christians, descendants of the ancient possessors of that country, may justly cast
off the Turkish yoke they have so long groaned under, whenever they have the opportunity? For no government can
have a right to obedience from a people who have not freely consented to it. This can never be supposed to happen
until they are put in a full state of liberty to choose their government and governors, or at least until they have
standing laws to which they or their representatives have given free consent, and until they are allowed their due
property—meaning they are proprietors of what they have such that no one can take any part of it without their
consent. Without this, men under any government are not in the state of freemen, but are direct slaves under the
force of war.

**Sect. 193.** But granting that the conqueror in a just war has a right to the estates as well as power over the
persons of the conquered—which, it is plain, he does not—nothing of absolute power will follow from this in
the continuance of the government. Because the descendants of these are all freemen, if he grants them estates
and possessions to inhabit his country (without which it would be worth nothing), whatever he grants them, they
have property in, so far as it is granted. The nature of property is that it cannot be taken from a man without
his own consent.

**Sect. 194.** Their persons are free by a native right, and their properties, however much or little, are their
own and at their own disposal, not his; otherwise it is not property.

Suppose the conqueror gives one man a thousand acres for him and his heirs forever; and to another, he lets a
thousand acres for his life, paying a rent of 50 or 500 pounds per year. Does the first not have a right to his
thousand acres forever, and the second for his life, provided he pays the rent? And does the tenant for life not
have property in everything he earns above his rent through his own labor and industry?

Can anyone say the king, or conqueror, after his grant, may use his power to take away all or part of the land from
the heirs of one, or from the other during his life? Or can he take away the goods or money they earned on that land
at his pleasure? If he can, then all free and voluntary contracts in the world are void. Nothing would be needed
to dissolve them but raw power. All grants and promises of men in power would be a mere mockery and collusion. For
what could be more ridiculous than to say, "I give you and yours this forever" in the most solemn way possible,
only for it to be understood that "I have the right, if I please, to take it away from you again tomorrow"?

**Sect. 195.** I will not dispute now whether princes are exempt from the laws of their country; but this I am
certain: they owe subjection to the laws of God and nature. No one, no power, can exempt them from the obligations
of that eternal law. Those laws are so great and strong in the case of promises that omnipotency itself is tied
by them. Grants, promises, and oaths are bonds that hold the Almighty. Whatever flatterers may say to princes
of the world—who, along with all their people, are in comparison to the great God but as a drop in the bucket,
or dust on the balance, inconsiderable, nothing!

**Sect. 196.** The short of the case in conquest is this: the conqueror, if he has a just cause, has a despotical
right over the persons of all who actually aided and concurred in the war against him, and a right to make up his
damage and cost out of their labor and estates, provided he does not injure the rights of any other.

Over the rest of the people—those who did not consent to the war—and over the children of the captives,
and their possessions, he has no power. Thus, by virtue of conquest, he can have no lawful title to dominion
over them, nor derive it to his posterity. He is an aggressor if he attempts to seize their properties, thereby
putting himself in a state of war against them. He has no better right of principality than Hingar or Hubba the
Danes had in England, or Spartacus would have had if he had conquered Italy; it is a yoke to be cast off as soon
as God gives those under its subjection the courage and opportunity to do so.

Thus, regardless of whatever title the kings of Assyria had over Judah by the sword, God assisted Hezekiah to
throw off the dominion of that conquering empire. And the lord was with Hezekiah, and he prospered; wherefore he
went forth, and he rebelled against the king of Assyria, and served him not (2 Kings xviii. 7).

From this it is plain that shaking off a power set over one by force, and not by right, is no offense before God,
even if it is called "rebellion," and even if promises and covenants obtained by force intervened. It is highly
probable to anyone who reads the story of Ahaz and Hezekiah that the Assyrians subdued Ahaz, deposed him, and made
Hezekiah king during his father's lifetime; and that Hezekiah, by agreement, had paid homage and tribute all this time.
