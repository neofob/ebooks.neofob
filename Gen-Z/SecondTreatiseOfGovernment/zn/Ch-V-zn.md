# CHAPTER V

## OF PROPERTY (THE TOTAL ASSETS: LIFE, LIBERTY, AND ESTATE)

**Sect. 25.** Whether we look at natural reason—which tells us that humans have a right to survive and thus to
food, drink, and other things nature provides—or revelation—which tells us how God gave the world to Adam,
Noah, and his sons—it's very clear that God (as King David says in Psal. cxv. 16) gave the earth to the children
of men; He gave it to humanity in common.

Now, given this, some people find it really difficult to explain how anyone can ever "own" something. I won't
just settle for saying that if it's hard to explain property based on the idea that God gave the world to Adam
and his descendants in common, it's literally impossible for anyone except one universal monarch to own anything
if God gave the world to Adam and his heirs in succession, excluding everyone else. Instead, I'll try to show how
people can come to own various parts of what God gave to humanity in common, and how this happens without needing
an explicit contract from every single "commoner."

**Sect. 26.** God, who gave the world to humans in common, also gave them reason to use it for the best possible
survival and convenience. The earth and everything in it is for the support and comfort of our beings. Even though
all the fruits and animals produced by nature belong to humanity in common—and nobody originally has a private
claim to them in their natural state—there must be a way to "appropriate" them before they can actually be
useful to any specific person. The fruit or venison that feeds a wild Indian (who uses no fences and is still a
"tenant in common") must become *his*—meaning a part of him that others no longer have a right to—before it
can actually support his life.

**Sect. 27.** Although the earth and all inferior creatures are common to all, every person has property in their
own person; nobody has a right to that but themselves. The labor of their body and the work of their hands are
properly theirs. Whatever they remove from the state nature provided—by mixing their labor with it and joining
something of their own to it—they make it their property. By removing it from the common state, this labor annexes
something to it that excludes the common right of others. Since this labor is the unquestionable property of the
laborer, nobody else can have a right to what it's joined to—at least as long as there's enough, and as good,
left in common for everyone else.

**Sect. 28.** Someone nourished by acorns they picked up or apples they gathered from the woods has definitely
appropriated them. Nobody can deny the nourishment is theirs. When did they become theirs? When they were
digested? When they were eaten? When they were boiled? When they were brought home? Or when they were picked up? It's
plain: if the first gathering didn't make them theirs, nothing else could. That labor created a distinction between
those items and the rest of the common stock; it added something more than nature (the common mother of all)
had done, and so they became a private right.

Would anyone say they had no right to those acorns because they didn't have the consent of all mankind? Was it
a robbery to assume something that belonged to everyone in common? If such consent were necessary, humans would
have starved despite the plenty God provided. We see in "commons" (which stay common by agreement) that taking
a part of the common and removing it from its natural state is what starts the property—without it, the common
is useless. And taking a piece doesn't depend on the consent of every single commoner. For example, the grass my
horse bites, the turf my servant cuts, or the ore I dig in a place where I have common rights, becomes my property
without anyone's specific permission. The labor I used to remove them from that common state fixed my property in them.

**Sect. 29.** If we required explicit consent from every commoner for any appropriation, children or servants
couldn't even cut the meat their father or master provided for them without assigning every single person their
specific part. Even if the water in a fountain belongs to everyone, who can doubt that the water in the pitcher
belongs only to the person who drew it out? Their labor took it from nature—where it was common—and appropriated
it to themselves.

**Sect. 30.** This logic of reason makes the deer belong to the Indian who killed it; it's his goods because he put
his labor into it, even though it was common before. Among "civilized" people who have created a ton of positive
laws to determine property, this original law of nature still applies. For instance, whatever fish anyone catches
in the ocean (the great remaining common) or whatever ambergris anyone finds is made their property by the labor
used to remove it from its natural state. Even the hare being hunted is thought to belong to the person pursuing
it; by employing labor to find and chase it, they've removed it from the state of nature and started a property.

**Sect. 31.** Some might object: "If gathering acorns or fruit creates a right to them, then anyone can just hoard
as much as they want." To that, I say: Not so. The same law of nature that gives us property also bounds it. God
gave us all things richly (1 Tim. vi. 12 is the voice of reason confirmed by inspiration). But for what purpose? To
enjoy. A person can only appropriate as much as they can actually use before it spoils; anything beyond that is
more than their share and belongs to others. God didn't make things for humans to waste or destroy. Given the
abundance of natural provisions and the relatively small number of people for a long time, one person's industry
couldn't really take away too much from others, especially when staying within the bounds of reason for their own
use. There was little room for property fights back then.

**Sect. 32.** But the main issue of property now isn't just fruit and animals, but the earth itself—since it
contains and carries everything else. Property in land is acquired the same way. As much land as a person tills,
plants, improves, cultivates, and can actually use the products of, becomes their property. By their labor, they
effectively "inclose" it from the common. It doesn't invalidate the right just because everyone else has an equal
title to it; they don't need the consent of all mankind to inclose land. God told humans to labor, and the need for
survival required it. God and reason commanded humans to "subdue the earth"—meaning improve it for the benefit
of life—by putting their own labor into it. Someone who obeys this command by tilling and sowing a piece of land
annexes something to it that was their own property, which others have no title to and cannot take without injury.

**Sect. 33.** This appropriation of land through improvement wasn't a prejudice to anyone else, because there
was still plenty of land—better and more than enough—left for anyone who hadn't used it yet. In effect, there
was never less left for others because of one person's enclosure. If you leave enough for another person to use,
you've basically taken nothing at all. Nobody feels robbed when another person takes a drink from a river if
there's a whole river of the same water left to quench their thirst; the case for land and water is exactly the same.

**Sect. 34.** God gave the world to humans in common, but since He gave it for our benefit and the greatest
conveniences of life, He didn't mean it should always stay common and uncultivated. He gave it to the industrious
and rational (and labor is their title to it), not to the fanciful or greedy. Someone who has just as much left for
their improvement as what's already been taken shouldn't complain or mess with what another's labor has already
improved. If they do, they're just trying to steal the benefit of another's pain, rather than using the common
ground God gave them, of which there was plenty.

**Sect. 35.** It's true that on "commons" in England or other countries with high populations and commercial systems,
nobody can inclose or appropriate land without the consent of all fellow-commoners. This is because those lands are
kept common by a compact (the law of the land), which cannot be violated. Also, such lands are the joint property
of a specific country or parish, not all mankind. In those cases, an enclosure would actually make the remaining
land less useful for the other commoners. But in the beginning, during the first populating of the world, it was
different. The law was about appropriation. God commanded it, and survival forced it. What a person cultivated
became their property. Subduing the earth and having dominion over it go together; the first gives title to the
second. God's command to subdue gave authority to appropriate. The needs of human life, which require labor and
materials, naturally introduce private possessions.

**Sect. 36.** Nature sets the limit of property by the extent of human labor and the needs of life. No one's
labor can subdue everything, nor can their enjoyment consume more than a small part. It was impossible for anyone
to cut into another's right or acquire property at their neighbor's expense, because there was always room for
others to have an equally large possession. This kept possessions moderate, especially in the early ages when
the biggest danger was getting lost in the wilderness rather than running out of room to plant. Even now, this
measure works without hurting anyone. If a family started planting in some vacant place in America, the amount
they could actually make use of wouldn't be that large, and it wouldn't give the rest of mankind any reason to
complain, even though humans have since spread everywhere. In fact, land is so low-value without labor that I've
heard that in Spain, a person can be allowed to plow and reap land they have no title to, just by using it. The
locals actually feel grateful to the person who improves wasted land and increases the food supply. Regardless,
the rule remains: everyone should have as much as they can make use of. There's enough land to support double the
current population if it weren't for the invention of money and the agreement to put a value on it, which allowed
for larger possessions. I'll explain that soon.

**Sect. 37.** In the beginning, before the desire for "more than enough" changed the intrinsic value of things
(which depends only on usefulness), humans had a right to appropriate as much as they could actually use. This
didn't hurt others since there was plenty for everyone. Furthermore, someone who appropriates land through labor
actually increases the common stock of humanity. For example, one acre of cultivated land produces roughly ten
times more (and actually closer to a hundred times more) than an acre of waste land. So, someone who incloses ten
acres and gets more from them than they would from a hundred acres of nature has effectively "given" ninety acres
back to mankind. If you compare a thousand acres of wild American woods to ten acres of well-cultivated land in
Devonshire, the ten acres provide far more convenience.

Before land was appropriated, whoever gathered the most wild fruit or caught the most animals—by putting in the
labor to change them from their natural state—acquired property in them. But if they let things rot or putrefy
without using them, they offended the law of nature and deserved punishment. They were stealing from their neighbor's
share, because they had no right to more than they could use. Hoarding more than you can use is just foolish and
dishonest. However, giving some away so it doesn't waste is fine. And bartering plums that would rot in a week for
nuts that last a year isn't an injury; it doesn't waste the common stock. Similarly, trading nuts for a shiny piece
of metal or sheep for diamonds doesn't invade anyone's rights, because these durable things don't spoil. A person can
hoard as much of these as they want; the limit isn't the size of the possession, but whether things are being wasted.

**Sect. 38.** The same measures apply to land. Whatever you till, reap, and use before it spoils is your
right. Whatever you inclose and can actually feed and use is yours. But if the grass in your enclosure rots or
the fruit perishes without being gathered, that part of the earth is still "waste" and can be taken by anyone
else. In the beginning, Cain could take as much ground as he could till, and still leave enough for Abel's sheep;
a few acres worked for both. As families grew and industry increased, so did their possessions. Eventually, they
settled and built cities, and by consent, they set out boundaries for their territories and made laws to settle
property within their society. In the earliest inhabited parts of the world, people wandered with their flocks
(like Abraham did), meaning most land was common and people only claimed what they actually used. When there wasn't
enough room for all the herds, they separated and expanded their pastures by agreement, like Abraham and Lot did.

**Sect. 39.** So, without assuming that Adam had a private dominion over the whole world (which can't be proved),
we can see how labor creates distinct titles to parcels of land for private use. This creates a clear right and
leaves no room for fighting.

**Sect. 40.** It might seem weird that the property of labor can outweigh the "community of land," but labor is what
actually creates value. Just look at the difference between an acre of land planted with tobacco, sugar, wheat,
or barley, and an acre of common land with zero husbandry. Labor creates the vast majority of the value. I'd say
a modest estimate is that 90% of all useful earth products are the result of labor. In fact, if you look at the
actual costs, 99% of things are purely the result of labor.

**Sect. 41.** The best proof of this is looking at some American nations who are rich in land but poor in the
comforts of life. Nature gave them a fruitful soil that could produce plenty of food and clothing, but because
they didn't improve it with labor, they have less than 1% of the conveniences we enjoy. A king of a huge, fertile
territory there lives, eats, and dresses worse than a day-laborer in England.

**Sect. 42.** To make it clearer, let's look at bread, wine, and cloth. If it weren't for labor, our bread would be
acorns, our drink would be water, and our clothes would be leaves or skins. The extra value of bread over acorns, wine
over water, and silk over moss is entirely due to labor and industry. The ground that provides the materials barely
counts toward the value; land left completely to nature is called "waste" because its benefit is practically zero.

This shows that having *more people* is better than having *larger territories*. The real art of government is
increasing the amount of land and using it correctly. A prince who is wise and godlike enough to use established
laws of liberty to protect and encourage honest industry against the oppression of power or narrow parties will
quickly outpace his neighbors. But that's a side note.

**Sect. 43.** An acre of land that produces twenty bushels of wheat in England and an acre that does the same in
America have the same "natural" value. But the benefit to humanity from the English acre is worth 5 pounds, while
the American one might not be worth a penny if it were valued and sold here. Labor puts the value on land. The
bread we eat is the result of the plowman's pain, the reaper's toil, the baker's sweat, and the labor of those
who made the oxen, the iron, the stones, and the timber for the mill and oven. Nature and the earth provided only
the basically worthless raw materials. The list of everything industry provides for a single loaf of bread—iron,
wood, leather, bricks, coal, cloth, dye, ship materials for the workers—would be way too long to list.

**Sect. 44.** So, it's evident that while nature's things are given in common, humans—by being masters of
themselves and owners of their own labor—have the foundation for property. Whatever they produce for their
support or comfort through invention and art is their own and doesn't belong in common.

**Sect. 45.** In the beginning, labor gave a right of property whenever someone used it on common things. For a
long time, this was the case for most of the world. People mostly lived on what nature offered. Later, in places
with more people and the use of money, land became scarce and valuable. Communities set boundaries and made laws
to regulate property, settling what labor and industry had started. International treaties that disown claims to
other countries' land also settled property by common consent. There are still huge tracts of land that stay common
because the inhabitants haven't joined the global economy (via money) and don't use all the land they have. This
rarely happens among people who use money.

**Sect. 46.** Most of the things truly useful for survival are short-lived; if you don't use them, they decay. Gold,
silver, and diamonds are valued based on fancy or agreement, not real use. Everyone has a right to as much as they
can use and property in everything they can create with their labor. Someone who gathers a hundred bushels of apples
owns them immediately. They just have to use them before they spoil, or they're stealing from others. Hoarding
more than you can use is foolish and dishonest. Giving some away or bartering perisables (like plums) for durables
(like nuts) is fine. Trading nuts for a shiny piece of metal or sheep for diamonds doesn't violate anyone's rights,
because these durable things don't waste. The limit isn't how much you possess, but whether you're letting things
perish uselessly.

**Sect. 47.** This is how money started: something durable that people agree to trade for the useful but perishable
supports of life.

**Sect. 48.** Since different levels of industry lead to different amounts of possessions, money gave people
the chance to expand. Imagine an island with 100 families and plenty of animals and land for 100,000 families,
but nothing that functions as money. There's no reason to own more land than your family needs, because there's
nothing to trade the extra for. Without something lasting and scarce to hoard, people won't expand their land,
no matter how rich it is. What's the point of owning 100,000 acres of cultivated land in America if you can't use
it for commerce to get money? It's not worth enclosing; you'd just give it back to nature.

**Sect. 49.** In the beginning, the whole world was like that—basically like America—because money didn't
exist. But as soon as people find something with the value of money, they'll start expanding their possessions.

**Sect. 50.** Gold and silver aren't very useful for survival compared to food and clothing; their value comes
from the consent of men (though labor still plays a part in that measure). People have agreed to an unequal
distribution of the earth by tacitly consenting to use money. This allows a person to fairly own more land than
they can personally use by trading the overplus for gold and silver, which can be hoarded without hurting anyone
since they don't decay. This unequal distribution was made possible without a formal contract just by valuing gold
and silver. In governments, however, the laws regulate property and land is determined by the constitution.

**Sect. 51.** So, it's easy to see how labor starts a title of property in common things, and how using those things
bounds that property. There's no reason to fight over titles or the size of possessions. Rights and convenience
align: a person has a right to everything they can labor on, and they have no temptation to labor for more than
they can actually use. This prevents controversy and encroachment. What a person carves out for themselves is easy
to see, and it's useless and dishonest to take more than you need.
