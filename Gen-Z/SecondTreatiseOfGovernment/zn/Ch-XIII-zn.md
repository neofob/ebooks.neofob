# CHAPTER XIII

## THE SUBORDINATION OF THE POWERS OF THE COMMONWEALTH

**Sect. 149.** In a functioning commonwealth, the legislative is the supreme power and everyone else is subordinate
to it. However, the legislative is only a "fiduciary" power (a trust) given for specific goals. If the legislative
acts against that trust, the people still have the supreme power to remove or change them. Since any power given
for a goal is limited by that goal, if that goal is ignored or actively fought, the trust is broken, and the power
goes back to the people, who can put it wherever they want for their own safety. The community always keeps the
ultimate power to save themselves from their own legislators if those legislators become foolish or wicked enough
to attack the liberties and assets of the subjects. Nobody can give away their right to self-preservation to the
absolute will of another. When someone tries to force them into a slave-like condition, the people have the right
to remove those who violate the sacred law of self-preservation. In this specific sense, the community is the
supreme power, but this only happens when the government is dissolved.

**Sect. 150.** As long as the government exists, the legislative is supreme. Whoever makes the laws for everyone
must be superior to those following the laws. Since the legislative makes rules for every member and controls
their execution, it's the top power, and all others are derived from and subordinate to it.

**Sect. 151.** In some commonwealths, the executive is a single person who also has a share in the legislative. In
a way, this person is also "supreme," not because they can make laws alone, but because they control the supreme
execution of the law. All other magistrates get their power from him. Since he is part of the legislative, he
isn't subject to a separate legislative power. He's sworn to obey the law, and if he violates it, he's no longer
acting as the "public person" but as a private individual with no right to be obeyed. He is effectively just a
representative of the commonwealth's will as declared in the laws.

**Sect. 152.** The executive power is subordinate and accountable to the legislative unless the executive also has
a share in the legislative. In that case, there's no separate superior. Other smaller ministerial powers are just
delegated roles and are accountable to whoever gave them the commission.

**Sect. 153.** The legislative doesn't need to be in session all the time, but the executive must be, because
laws already made always need to be enforced. Whenever the legislative puts the execution of laws in other hands,
they can always take that power back if they find maladministration. If the legislative consists of a group, they
can meet whenever the original constitution says, or whenever they want (unless they've adjourned to a specific
time). Since the people gave them this power, they can exercise it whenever they please.

**Sect. 154.** If the legislative is made of representatives chosen for a set time, the people must be able to choose
them again at regular intervals or when called. Usually, the executive handles the calling of elections—either
by following a fixed schedule or by using prudence to call them when public needs require updating laws or fixing
problems.

**Sect. 155.** What if the executive uses the society's force to stop the legislative from meeting? Using force
without authority and against the public trust is a state of war against the people. The people then have the right
to restore their legislative by force. The only remedy for unauthorized force is to oppose it with force. Using
force without authority makes the user an aggressor and liable to be treated as such.

**Sect. 156.** The executive's power to assemble and dismiss the legislative isn't a sign of superiority, but a
trust for the safety of the people. Since the future is unpredictable, fixed meeting times for the legislature
might be too rigid or too burdensome for the people. Having a leader who is always present to watch the public
good and call meetings when necessary is the best remedy. As long as the executive uses this prerogative for the
public good, it's just a tool.

**Sect. 157.** The world is always changing—people, wealth, and power shift. Mighty cities fall, and empty places
grow into huge populations. Sometimes, old customs and privileges stick around even after the reasons for them
are gone. In representative governments, this can lead to "rotten boroughs" where a ghost town with one shepherd
sends as many representatives to the assembly as a whole county full of people. This is a huge absurdity. Since the
legislative is the supreme act of the society and depends entirely on the people, no lower power can fix this. Many
believe this means the problem is unsolvable as long as the government stands.

**Sect. 158.** The rule "the safety of the people is the supreme law" (*Salus populi suprema lex*) is always
correct. If the executive uses the power of convoking the legislative to fix representation (making it based on actual
population/assistance rather than old custom), they aren't creating a new legislative; they're just restoring the
original, true one. Anyone who helps the people get fair representation is a friend to the government. Prerogative
is simply the power to do a "good thing" when there's no specific rule for it. Any action that clearly benefits
the society and establishes government on its true foundations is just prerogative.

**Sect. 159.** (Included in previous chapters/texts but maintained in logical flow: la prerogative is the latitude
given to the executive to act for the public good where the law is silent or where a strict application of the
law would actually cause harm).
