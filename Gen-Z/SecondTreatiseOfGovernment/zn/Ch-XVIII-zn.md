# CHAPTER XVIII

## OF TYRANNY

**Sect. 199.** Just as usurpation is the act of exercising power that belongs to someone else, tyranny is the act
of exercising power *beyond* any right—a move that no one can ever legitimately justify. Tyranny happens when
someone uses the power they hold not for the benefit of the people under them, but for their own private, selfish
gain. It occurs when a governor, regardless of how they got their title, stops following the law and starts making
their own will the rule; when their commands and actions aren't about protecting the people's Property (the total
assets: life, liberty, and estate), but are instead driven by ambition, revenge, greed, or any other unstable passion.

**Sect. 200.** If anyone doubts this is truth or reason because it comes from the "obscure hand of a subject,"
I hope the authority of a king will make it slide. King James I, in his 1603 speech to parliament, told them:

> "I will always prefer the public good, and the wealth of the whole commonwealth, in making good laws and
constitutions, over any of my own private ends; believing that the wealth and well-being of the commonwealth is
my own greatest happiness. This is exactly where a lawful king differs from a tyrant: for I acknowledge that the
main difference between a rightful king and a usurping tyrant is this: while the proud and ambitious tyrant thinks
his kingdom and people exist only to satisfy his desires and unreasonable appetites, the righteous and just king
acknowledges that he was appointed specifically to secure the wealth and property of his people."

And again, in his 1609 speech to parliament, he said:

> "The king binds himself by a double oath to follow the fundamental laws of his kingdom: implicitly, by being a
king, he is bound to protect both the people and the laws; and explicitly, by his coronation oath. Every just king
in a settled kingdom is bound to observe the pact made with his people through the laws, framing his government to
be agreeable to that pact—much like the covenant God made with Noah after the deluge: that seed-time and harvest,
cold and heat, summer and winter, day and night, shall not cease as long as the earth remains. Therefore, a king
governing a settled kingdom stops being a king and degenerates into a tyrant the moment he stops ruling according
to the laws."

Shortly after, he added:

> "Therefore, all kings who are not tyrants or perjurers will be happy to stay within the limits of their laws;
and those who persuade them otherwise are vipers and pests to both the king and the commonwealth."

Thus, this learned king, who truly understood how things work, makes the difference between a king and a tyrant
simple: one makes the laws the boundary of his power and the public good the goal of his government; the other
lets everything take a backseat to his own will and appetite.

**Sect. 201.** It would be a mistake to think this flaw only exists in monarchies; other forms of government are just
as susceptible. Wherever power—put into someone's hands for the government of the people and the preservation of
their properties—is used for other ends, or used to impoverish, harass, or subdue the people through arbitrary
and irregular commands, it immediately becomes tyranny, whether the people in power are one person or many. We
read of the "Thirty Tyrants" in Athens as well as one in Syracuse, and the intolerable rule of the Decemviri in
Rome was no different.

**Sect. 202.** Wherever law ends, tyranny begins—if the law is broken to another's harm. Anyone in authority
who exceeds the power given to them by the law, using the force under their command to do to a subject what the
law does not allow, ceases to be a magistrate in that moment. Acting without authority, they can be opposed just
like any other person who uses force to invade the rights of another.

This is already accepted for lower-level officials. A man who has the authority to arrest me in the street can be
opposed as a thief and a robber if he tries to break into my house to execute that warrant, even if I know he has
the legal authority to arrest me in public. Why shouldn't this same logic apply to the highest magistrate as well
as the most inferior?

Is it reasonable that an oldest brother, just because he inherited the biggest part of the estate, has the right
to take away his younger brothers' portions? Or that a rich man who owns an entire country has the right to seize
his poor neighbor's cottage and garden whenever he feels like it? Being rightfully possessed of great power and
wealth is not an excuse—and certainly not a reason—for robbery and oppression. In fact, harming another without
authority is actually *worse* when you have power. Exceeding the bounds of authority is no more a "right" for a
high-ranking official than for a petty one; it is no more justifiable for a king than for a constable. It is simply
worse for the king, because he has more trust placed in him, already possesses a much larger share than his brethren,
and is expected—thanks to his education, position, and advisors—to be more aware of what is right and wrong.

**Sect. 203.** But wait—can the commands of a prince actually be opposed? Can he be resisted every time someone
feels aggrieved or *imagines* they've been treated unfairly? Doing that would unhinge and overturn all political
structures, replacing government and order with nothing but anarchy and confusion.

**Sect. 204.** To this I answer: force is to be opposed to *nothing* but unjust and unlawful force. Anyone who
opposes something other than that brings a just condemnation upon themselves from both God and man. Therefore,
the danger or confusion often suggested will not follow, because:

**Sect. 205.** First, in some countries, the person of the prince is legally sacred. Whatever he commands or does,
his person remains free from question or violence, not liable to force or judicial censure. However, opposition
can still be made to the illegal acts of his inferior officers or anyone commissioned by him. Unless the prince
actually puts himself into a state of war with his people—thereby dissolving the government and leaving them to the
self-defense that belongs to everyone in the state of nature (who can predict the end of such a thing? A neighboring
kingdom once gave the world a strange example)—the sacredness of the prince's person protects him from all harm.

This is a wise constitution. The harm he can do in his own person is unlikely to happen often or extend far;
he cannot single-handedly subvert the laws or oppress the entire body of the people. If any prince is so weak or
ill-natured that he wants to try, the occasional private mischiefs are a fair trade-off for the public peace and
the security of the government provided by having the chief magistrate out of reach of danger. It is safer for the
collective body that a few individuals might occasionally suffer than for the head of the republic to be easily
and trivially exposed.

**Sect. 206.** Secondly, this privilege belongs only to the king's *person*. It does not stop people from questioning,
opposing, and resisting those who use unjust force, even if they claim a commission from the king that the law
does not authorize.

For example, someone might have a king's writ to arrest a man—a full commission from the king. Yet, that person
cannot break open a man's house to do it, nor can they execute the command on certain days or in certain places,
even if the commission doesn't explicitly say so. Those are the limitations of the *law*, and if anyone breaks
them, the king's commission does not excuse them. Since the king's authority is given to him only by the law,
he cannot authorize anyone to act *against* the law. A commission or command from a magistrate where they have no
authority is as void and meaningless as that of any private citizen. The only difference is that the magistrate
has some authority for certain ends, while the private citizen has none. It is the *authority*, not the piece of
paper (the commission), that gives the right to act; and there can be no authority against the laws. Despite such
resistance, the king's person and authority remain secure, so there is no danger to the governor or the government.

**Sect. 207.** Thirdly, even in a government where the chief magistrate's person is not sacred, this doctrine of
resisting unlawful power will not endanger him or throw the government into chaos on every slight occasion. Where
the injured party can be relieved and their damages repaired by appealing to the law, there is no justification
for force. Force is only to be used when a man is blocked from appealing to the law. Nothing is "hostile force"
unless it removes the remedy of such an appeal; and it is *that* kind of force alone that puts the user into a
state of war and makes it lawful to resist them.

If a man with a sword demands my purse on the highway, and I happen to have only twelve pence in my pocket, I
may lawfully kill him. But if I give 100 pounds to another man to hold while I get off a horse, and he refuses to
give it back and draws his sword to defend it by force, the mischief he does me is a hundred or a thousand times
greater than what the highwayman intended (whom I killed before he actually did me any harm)—and yet I might
lawfully kill the first man, but I cannot even legally hurt the second.

The reason is simple: the first man used force that threatened my life, so I had no time to appeal to the law to
save it; once my life was gone, it was too late to appeal. The law cannot restore life to a corpse. The loss was
irreparable, which is why the law of nature gave me the right to destroy someone who puts himself into a state of
war with me and threatens my destruction. In the second case, my life is not in danger, so I can use the benefit
of appealing to the law to get my 100 pounds back.

**Sect. 208.** Fourthly, even if the unlawful acts of a magistrate are maintained by the power he holds, and the
legal remedy is blocked by that same power, the right of resisting even such manifest acts of tyranny will not
suddenly disturb the government on slight occasions. If it only affects a few private individuals, they have a
right to defend themselves and recover by force what was taken from them; but they will not easily enter a contest
where they are sure to perish. It is as impossible for one or a few oppressed men to disturb a government—if
the general public does not feel concerned—as it is for a raving madman or a random malcontent to overturn a
well-settled state. The people are just as unlikely to follow them.

**Sect. 209.** But if these illegal acts extend to the majority of the people, or if the oppression hits only a
few but creates a precedent and consequences that threaten everyone—and they are convinced in their hearts that
their laws, estates, liberties, lives, and perhaps even their religion are in danger—how could they be stopped
from resisting illegal force?

I admit this is an inconvenience that attends all governments when the governors have let things get to the point
where they are generally suspected by their people. This is the most dangerous state a governor can put themselves
in. They deserve little pity because it is so easy to avoid; it is just as impossible for a governor who truly
means the good and preservation of his people and their laws NOT to make them feel it, as it is for a father not
to let his children see that he loves and cares for them.

**Sect. 210.** But if the whole world sees a pattern of one kind of pretense and another kind of action—arts used
to dodge the law, and the "trust of prerogative" (which is arbitrary power left in the prince's hand to do good,
not harm) used contrary to its purpose—the people will notice. If they see that the ministers and subordinate
magistrates are chosen based on whether they promote these ends; if they see several experiments with arbitrary
power, and a certain religion secretly favored while publicly denounced; if a long train of actions shows that
the councils are all heading in that direction—how can a man stop himself from seeing where things are going?

How could he stop himself from trying to save himself, any more than he could stop himself from believing the
captain of his ship was sailing him and everyone else to Algiers, when he finds the captain *always* steering that
course? Even if crosswinds, leaks, and a lack of men and food occasionally force the captain to turn away for a
while, he steadily returns to that course as soon as the wind and weather allow.
