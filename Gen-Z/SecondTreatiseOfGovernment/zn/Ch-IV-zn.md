# CHAPTER IV

## OF SLAVERY

**Sect. 22.** Natural liberty basically means being free from any superior power on earth; you aren't under anyone's
will or legislative authority, and the only rule you follow is the law of nature. In a society, liberty means
you're only under a legislative power that was established by consent in the commonwealth. You aren't under the
whim of any individual or the restraint of any law except those the legislature enacts based on the trust put in it.

Freedom isn't what Sir Robert Filmer tells us—that it's just liberty for everyone to do whatever they want,
live however they please, and not be tied by any laws. Actual freedom under government is having a consistent
rule for everyone in that society, created by the legislative power. It's the liberty to follow your own will in
everything the rule doesn't cover, and not being subject to the erratic, uncertain, unknown, or arbitrary will of
another person. Freedom in nature is simply being under no other restraint but the law of nature.

**Sect. 23.** This freedom from absolute, arbitrary power is so core to a person's survival that they can't give it
up without essentially giving up their life too. Since a person doesn't "own" their own life (in the sense of having
the right to destroy it), they can't—by contract or consent—enslave themselves to anyone, nor put themselves
under the absolute, arbitrary power of another to kill them whenever that person feels like it. You can't give
away more power than you actually have; since you can't take your own life, you can't give someone else power over it.

Now, if someone's actually forfeited their life through an act that deserves death, the person who holds them may delay
the execution and make them work as a servant. This isn't an injury, because whenever the slave feels the hardship
of slavery outweighs the value of their life, they can just resist and draw upon the death they already deserve.

**Sect. 24.** This is the "perfect" state of slavery, which is really just a state of war continued between a
lawful conqueror and a captive. If a compact is made between them—an agreement for limited power on one side
and obedience on the other—the state of war and slavery ends as long as the agreement lasts. As mentioned,
no one can agree to pass over a power they don't possess: the power over their own life.

I'll admit, we see that among the Jews and other nations, people did "sell" themselves. But it's clear this was
just for labor, not actual slavery. The person sold wasn't under absolute, arbitrary, despotic power. The master
couldn't just kill them at any time, because the master was eventually obliged to set them free. The master was
so far from having arbitrary power over their life that he couldn't even maim them—the loss of an eye or a tooth
actually set the servant free (Exod. xxi).
