# CHAPTER VIII

## THE BEGINNING OF POLITICAL SOCIETIES

**Sect. 95.** Since humans are naturally free, equal, and independent, nobody can be forced into the political
power of another without their own consent. The only way to trade natural liberty for the bonds of civil society
is to agree with others to join a community for a safer, more comfortable, and peaceful life, where they can
securely enjoy their properties and be protected from outsiders. Any number of people can do this without hurting
the freedom of those who choose to stay in the State of Nature. Once a group consents to form a government, they
are incorporated into one body politic, and the majority has the right to make decisions for the rest.

**Sect. 96.** When individuals consent to form a community, they create a single body that acts according to the
will of the majority. Since the community is based on the consent of the individuals, and a single body must move
in one direction, it has to follow the greater force—the majority. Otherwise, the community wouldn't be able to
act as one body. This is why in official assemblies, the act of the majority is the act of the whole.

**Sect. 97.** By joining a body politic, every person obligates themselves to submit to the majority. If they were
still free to ignore any decree they didn't personally like, the "compact" would be meaningless. It would just be
the same liberty they had in the State of Nature.

**Sect. 98.** If the majority's decision isn't accepted as the act of the whole, then any action would require 100%
unanimity. That's impossible in practice due to illness, business, different opinions, and conflicting interests. A
society based on total unanimity would dissolve almost immediately. Rational people wouldn't create societies just
to have them instantly fall apart.

**Sect. 99.** Anyone leaving the State of Nature to unite into a community must be understood to give up the
necessary power to the majority (unless they explicitly agree on a different number). Simply agreeing to unite
into one political society is the only compact needed. The start of any lawful government is just the consent of
free people to incorporate into such a society.

**Sect. 100.** There are two common objections to this. First, that history doesn't show independent, equal men
meeting and starting governments this way. Second, that it's impossible because everyone is born into a government
and must submit to it.

**Sect. 101.** To the first: it's not surprising that history doesn't mention people in the state of nature much. The
hardships of that life and the desire for society mean that as soon as people got together, they united. If we
can't assume people were ever in the state of nature because there aren't many records, we might as well assume
the great armies of ancient kings were never children. Government usually predates written records. Most people
only start researching their origins after their society has become stable. Most early polities are either clear
examples of voluntary union or show strong evidence of it.

**Sect. 102.** It's a weird move to deny facts just because they don't fit your theory. The beginnings of Rome
and Venice were formed by the union of free, independent people with no natural superiority. Josephus Acosta
also noted that in many parts of America, there was no formal government; people lived in troops and chose their
captains as needed. Even if they were subject to their fathers, that didn't stop them from uniting into a political
society. These societies all began from voluntary union and mutual agreement.

**Sect. 103.** I'm sure those who left Sparta with Palantus would be considered free men who set up their own
government by consent. I've given plenty of historical examples. If the lack of records is an argument against
this, then the people arguing for "paternal empire" should probably just give up. In fact, if they looked too
closely at how most governments actually started, they'd find very little to support their design.

**Sect. 104.** With reason on our side and historical examples showing that peaceful governments started with the
consent of the people, there's little room for doubt about how lawful governments are erected. (I'll talk about
conquest later).

**Sect. 105.** I won't deny that most early commonwealths were led by one man. In small, isolated families,
government often started with the father. Since the father already had the natural power to punish offenders, the
children—even as adults—usually submitted to him. He was the most trusted person, and the habit of obeying him
since childhood made it easy. If they needed a ruler, the father was the most obvious choice—unless he was cruel
or unfit. When the father died and the heir was unfit, or when multiple families joined, they used their natural
freedom to pick the ablest person to rule them. This is seen in the Americas, where they generally prefer the heir
of a king but will pass him over for the bravest man if the heir is weak.

**Sect. 106.** Early governments were often in one hand, but that doesn't mean government is naturally
monarchical. People probably chose one ruler at first because they were used to their father's rule from childhood,
and they found a single, competent leader efficient for their needs. Because they hadn't experienced tyranny and
lived simple lives with few property disputes, they didn't think to create checks and balances. They just needed
defense against foreign invasions.

**Sect. 107.** The kings of the ancient Americas are a great example. Since there wasn't much money or competition
for land, their kings were basically just generals. They had absolute power in war, but at home, the decisions
were made by the people or a council.

**Sect. 10H.** In early Israel, the judges and first kings were mainly army captains. Jephtha was made head and
captain to fight the Ammonites. Gideon was a judge because he fought the Midianites. Saul was anointed to be a
captain to save Israel from the Philistines. Even when David took over, the focus was on his ability to lead the
army. When the tribes submitted to David, they reminded him that he had already been their general under Saul. In
those days, "king" basically meant "general."

**Sect. 110.** Whether a family grew into a commonwealth or separate families joined together, the result was
often a single ruler. This happened because people trusted their fathers, or they needed a strong general for
protection. In the early stages, these rulers were trusted to act for the public good. Without such caretakers,
early societies would have collapsed.

**Sect. 111.** In the "golden age," people had more virtue and better governors, and there was no fighting over
"prerogative" or "privilege." But as vanity, luxury, and ambition grew in later ages, princes started having
interests separate from their people. This made it necessary to examine the original rights of government and find
ways to restrain the abuses of power that people had entrusted to others for their own good.

**Sect. 112.** So, people who were naturally free used their consent to put rule in one man's hand, usually because
it felt safe and efficient. They didn't believe in "divine right"—that's a recent invention. They just used
their reason. All peaceful governments started with the consent of the people.

**Sect. 113.** The other objection is that everyone is born under some government and therefore can't be free to
start a new one. If that were true, how did any lawful monarchy ever start? If one person born under a ruler is
free to start a new empire, then everyone born under a ruler is equally free to do so. Otherwise, there's only
one lawful government in the world.

**Sect. 114.** Everyone is born a subject to a father or prince, but that doesn't mean they are under a perpetual
tie of subjection without their own consent. History is full of examples of people withdrawing their obedience and
starting new governments. This is how many small kingdoms were formed. If paternal sovereignty were a natural right,
there would only be one universal monarchy.

**Sect. 116.** Being born into an ancient polity with established laws doesn't make you a natural subject any
more than being born in the woods does. Some argue that since our ancestors gave up their liberty, we are bound
to the same government. While a person is bound by the promises they make for themselves, they cannot bind their
children. A son, once an adult, is as free as his father. A father can attach conditions to the land he leaves
his son, but that's a property issue, not a natural tie.

**Sect. 117.** Most governments don't let their territory be broken up, so a son usually has to accept the same
terms as his father to keep the land. This makes them members of the society. Since this happens as each person
reaches adulthood, people often mistake it for a natural subjection.

**Sect. 118.** Governments know this. They don't claim power over a son just because they had power over the
father. For example, if an Englishman and English woman have a child in France, the child is not automatically a
subject of England or France. A child is born a subject of no country. They are under their father's care until
the age of discretion, and then they are free to choose which body politic to join.

**Sect. 119.** Since humans are naturally free and only subject to power through consent, we need to define
"consent." There's "express consent," which makes someone a full member. But there's also "tacit consent." I argue
that anyone who enjoys any part of a government's territory—whether they own land, rent a room, or just walk on
the highway—gives their tacit consent to obey the laws while they are there.

**Sect. 120.** When someone first joins a commonwealth, they also submit their possessions to it. You can't join
a society to regulate property but then claim your own land is exempt from the society's laws. So, by joining,
you unite your person and your possessions to the government. Anyone who later enjoys that land must accept the
condition of submitting to the government.

**Sect. 121.** Since the government's jurisdiction over a non-member only reaches them as long as they enjoy the
land, this obligation ends when the enjoyment ends. A person who only gave tacit consent is free to leave and join
another society. But someone who gave *express* consent is permanently bound unless the government dissolves.

**Sect. 122.** Just living in a country and enjoying its protection doesn't make you a "member" in the permanent
sense. It's like staying in someone's house for a while; you follow the house rules, but you aren't family. Foreigners
are bound to obey the laws while they are there, but they aren't subjects unless they make a positive, express compact.
