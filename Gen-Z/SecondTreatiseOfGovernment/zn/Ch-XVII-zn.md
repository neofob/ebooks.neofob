# CHAPTER XVII

## OF USURPATION

**Sect. 197.** Just as conquest can be viewed as a foreign usurpation, usurpation itself is essentially a kind of
domestic conquest. The key difference is that a usurper can *never* have the right on his side, because it only
qualifies as usurpation when someone takes possession of something another person has a legitimate right to. As
long as it remains strictly usurpation, it is merely a change of persons, not a change in the forms and rules of
the government. However, if the usurper extends his power beyond what rightfully belonged to the lawful princes
or governors of the commonwealth, then it is no longer just usurpation—it is tyranny added to usurpation.

**Sect. 198.** In all lawful governments, designating the specific persons who are to rule is as natural and necessary
a part of the system as the form of government itself. This designation is what was originally established by the
people. To have a government with no way to designate its leader is basically the same as having no government at
all; it would be like agreeing that a system should be monarchical, but failing to appoint any method for choosing
the person who actually holds the power and serves as the monarch.

Hence, every commonwealth that has an established form of government also has rules for appointing those who share
in public authority and settled methods for conveying that right to them. Whoever assumes any part of that power
through means other than what the laws of the community have prescribed has no right to be obeyed, even if the
general form of the commonwealth is still preserved. Since he is not the person the laws have appointed, he is
consequently not the person the people have consented to. Neither such a usurper, nor anyone deriving authority
from him, can ever hold a legitimate title until the people are both free to consent and have actually consented
to allow and confirm the power he had previously usurped.
