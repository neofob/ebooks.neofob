# CHAPTER III

## OF THE STATE OF WAR

**Sect. 16.** The State of War is a state of pure enmity and destruction. If someone—not just in a moment of
heat or a hasty reaction, but with a calm, settled plan—decides to come for another person's life, they've
officially put themselves in a state of war with that person. By doing so, they've exposed their own life to the
other's power: it can be taken by the victim or anyone who joins their defense and backs their struggle. It's only
reasonable and just that I should have the right to destroy that which threatens me with destruction. According to
the fundamental law of nature, humans should be preserved as much as possible; but when not everyone can be saved,
the safety of the innocent comes first. One can take out someone who makes war on them, or has revealed a hatred
for their existence, for the same reason one might kill a wolf or a lion. Such people aren't bound by the common
laws of reason; they only follow the rule of force and violence. They are effectively beasts of prey—dangerous,
toxic creatures who will absolutely destroy you the moment you fall into their power.

**Sect. 17.** Because of this, anyone who tries to force another person into their absolute power has effectively
declared war on them. This is basically a announcement of a plan for their life. I have every reason to conclude
that someone who wants to force me into their power without my consent would just use me however they liked once
they had me, and would destroy me the moment they felt like it. Nobody wants me in their absolute power unless
it's to force me into something that violates the right of my freedom—which is just a fancy way of saying they
want to make me a slave. Being free from that kind of force is my only real security. Reason tells me that anyone
who tries to take away the freedom that protects my existence is an enemy to my survival; so, anyone who tries to
enslave me is officially in a state of war with me. Someone in the State of Nature (the OG state) who tries to take
away another's freedom is necessarily planning to take away everything else, because freedom is the foundation for
all other rights. Similarly, anyone in a society who tries to strip away the freedom of its members is planning
to take everything else and should be viewed as being in a state of war.

**Sect. 18.** This is why it's lawful to kill a thief, even if they haven't actually hurt you yet or explicitly said
they want to kill you—beyond the fact that they're using force to get you into their power so they can take your
money or whatever else they want. By using force where they have no right to do so, regardless of their excuse,
I have no reason to believe that someone who would steal my liberty wouldn't also steal everything else once they
had me. Therefore, it's lawful for me to treat them as someone in a state of war with me—which means I can kill
them if I can. That's the risk they take whenever they start a state of war and act as the aggressor.

**Sect. 19.** This highlights the clear difference between the State of Nature (the OG state) and the State of
War. Some people confuse the two, but they are as different as a state of peace, goodwill, and mutual help is
from a state of malice, violence, and mutual destruction. People living together according to reason, without a
common superior on earth to judge between them, is properly the State of Nature (the OG state). But force—or
a declared plan to use force—against another person, where there's no common superior to appeal to for help,
is the State of War.

The lack of such an appeal is what gives a person the right of war even against an aggressor who is a fellow
citizen in a society. For example, a thief who has stolen everything I own—whom I normally couldn't touch without
appealing to the law—can be killed the moment he tries to rob me of my horse or my coat. The law, which exists
for my preservation, permits me my own defense and the right of war (the liberty to kill the aggressor) when it
cannot step in to secure my life from immediate force. If my life is lost, there's no reparation possible. The
aggressor doesn't give me time to appeal to a common judge or wait for a legal decision. A lack of a common judge
with authority puts all people in a State of Nature (the OG state); but force without right against a person's
life creates a State of War, whether a common judge exists or not.

**Sect. 20.** Once the actual force is over, the state of war ends between people in a society who are both subject
to the fair determination of the law. This is because they can then appeal to the law for past injuries and prevent
future harm. But where no such appeal exists—as in the State of Nature (the OG state), due to a lack of positive
laws and authorized judges—the state of war continues once it's started. The innocent party has the right to
destroy the other whenever they can, until the aggressor offers peace and wants to reconcile on terms that repair
the wrongs already done and secure the innocent for the future.

Furthermore, even where an appeal to the law and judges is officially open, if the remedy is denied by a blatant
perversion of justice—where the laws are flat-out twisted to protect the violence of certain people—it's hard to
see that as anything other than a state of war. Wherever violence is used and injury is done, even by those appointed
to administer justice, it's still violence and injury, regardless of the legal name or form it's given. The point
of the law is to protect and help the innocent through an unbiased application to everyone. Wherever that isn't
happening in good faith, war is’ made upon the sufferers. Since they have no appeal on earth to set things right,
they are left with the only remedy in such cases: an appeal to heaven.

**Sect. 21.** Avoiding this state of war (where there's no appeal but to heaven, and where the tiniest difference
can end in disaster because there's no authority to decide) is one of the biggest reasons people join a society
and leave the State of Nature (the OG state). Where there is an authority—a power on earth that can provide
relief through appeal—the state of war is stopped and the controversy is decided by that power. If there had
been a court or a superior jurisdiction on earth to settle the right between Jephtha and the Ammonites, they would
never have gone to war; but Jephtha was forced to appeal to heaven. He said, "The Lord the Judge be judge this
day between the children of Israel and the children of Ammon" (Judg. xi. 27), and then he led his army to battle.

In these cases, when people ask "who shall be judge?", it doesn't mean "who will decide the controversy?"—everyone
knows from Jephtha that the Lord the Judge will decide. The real question is: "am I the one who gets to judge whether
another person has put themselves in a state of war with me, and whether I can appeal to heaven?" Only I can be
the judge of that in my own conscience, and I'll answer for it on the great day before the supreme judge of all humans.
